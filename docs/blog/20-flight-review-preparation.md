# A flight review that addresses the flying you actually do

Plan your flight review around your recent flying, the trips you want to make and the skills you haven't practiced. Give the instructor that picture before the appointment. You'll have a better chance of spending the session on decisions and handling that need attention, while still covering the required material.

A review can uncover a gap that routine flights keep hidden. Perhaps you land comfortably at your home airport but avoid unfamiliar airspace. Perhaps you know the navigator well until the clearance changes. The preparation starts with naming that gap clearly enough for an instructor to assess it.

An [instructor's question about how to conduct a flight review](https://www.reddit.com/r/CFILounge/comments/1tegwy5/how_do_you_treat_a_flight_review/) captures the uncertainty over evaluation versus teaching. This US-first guide uses current FAA rules and guidance, checked on **22 October 2026**. The inspected eCFR pages display Title 14 as updated through 20 October; the pilot profiles below are original fictional examples.

## Separate the requirement from the training goal

Under the ordinary airplane-review route, [§61.56(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.56) requires at least one hour of flight training and one hour of ground training, subject to the section's exceptions. The review includes current Part 91 operating rules and the maneuvers and procedures the person giving it considers necessary to demonstrate safe exercise of your pilot privileges. Those are minimum times, not a promised two-hour completion package.

For the usual review-and-endorsement route, §61.56(c) defines the calendar-month period and requires satisfactory completion in an aircraft for which you are rated, with the authorized instructor's logbook endorsement. As an **original calendar example**, a review completed in October 2024 can fall within that period for an October 2026 flight; it falls outside it for a November 2026 flight. That example assumes no other qualifying event and doesn't settle your other qualifications.

The rule also recognizes specified practical tests, proficiency checks and FAA pilot-proficiency-program phases. Have the instructor check which provision applies to your records. Completing an arbitrary online course isn't the same as completing a qualifying program phase.

The endorsement alone doesn't establish passenger-carrying, night or instrument recency. Those need a separate check under [§61.57](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.57), along with the other requirements for your proposed operation. Some flying during a review may contribute to a particular recency requirement, but the actual tasks and conditions must meet that requirement.

## Describe your recent flying honestly

Give the instructor more than your lifetime total. List when you last flew, the aircraft you have used recently, the kinds of trips you completed and the conditions you deliberately avoided. Include a change in panel, home airport or operating environment even if you have many hours in the basic aircraft model.

Separate frequent exposure from recent practice. Twenty comfortable trips to familiar airports may provide less evidence about diversion planning than one recent session devoted to it. An untouched checklist item isn't proof that the skill has disappeared; it's a reason to assess it rather than guess.

The FAA's active [AC 61-98E](https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentID/1043281), dated 30 October 2024, recommends tailoring the review to the pilot's experience and intended flying. Its Chapter 4 addresses planning and debriefing; Appendices D–F provide example aids. The circular is guidance, while §61.56 supplies the rule.

Write down a few specific moments you want to discuss. “I get behind the airplane when I change destinations” gives the instructor something to investigate. “I should probably practice more” leaves the weak point undefined.

## Send the instructor a focused brief

Use the [flight-review preparation sheet](/blog/flight-review-preparation-worksheet.md) to send a short summary before the session. Include the relevant logbook entries, your proposed aircraft and a representative flight. Ask which aircraft documents, preparation exercises and current references the instructor wants you to bring.

Confirm that the instructor is appropriately qualified and familiar with the aircraft and equipment for the work you propose. Discuss how the session will be conducted, the criteria for satisfactory completion, expected fees and what happens if further training is needed. An agreement on scope makes a more productive starting point than an expectation that the clock guarantees an endorsement.

If your review period has elapsed, tell the instructor before arranging the flight. Agree who will act as pilot in command and how any repositioning or training flight will be conducted legally. Don't assume you can fly to the appointment as pilot in command simply because you intend to complete a review after arriving.

Also disclose a long absence from flying, an unfamiliar aircraft or a major planned mission change. The instructor may recommend a broader refresher or separate transition training. That can change the booking and aircraft choice before either of you spends time on the apron.

## Prepare one trip you might actually fly

Pick a representative trip and prepare the information you would need for it. Use current charts and weather information, the aircraft's approved data and your intended load. Give the instructor your reasoning, including the conditions that would make you delay, divert or choose another destination.

[§91.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.103) addresses preflight information, including weather, fuel and alternatives for the flights covered by its paragraph (a), and runway lengths and applicable performance information for any flight. A review discussion can expose whether you can find, interpret and apply that information. It can also expose where a familiar rule of thumb has replaced an aircraft-specific calculation.

For example, bring the departure loading calculation and ask the instructor to change one input. What happens if the second passenger is heavier, the departure is delayed into warmer conditions or the intended fuel stop becomes unavailable? Keep the [mission-loading guide](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) and [density-altitude article](/blog/density-altitude-takeoff-climb-performance) beside the actual aircraft data when preparing those questions.

The scenario needn't become the route flown during the review. Weather, traffic, aircraft status and the instructor's assessment may change the plan. The preparation still gives you a concrete way to discuss judgment rather than recite disconnected facts.

## Connect weak areas to an instructor-led plan

Turn each concern into something the instructor can observe. If you rush a diversion, ask for assessment of how you keep flying the airplane while selecting and briefing another destination. If automation distracts you, ask the instructor to assess the installed system and your ability to manage the flight when its expected assistance is unavailable.

Agree the safety boundaries and the instructor's role before any simulated problem. The instructor should select appropriate tasks, conditions and interventions for the aircraft and pilot. This article doesn't prescribe a maneuver sequence, a minimum practice altitude, an engine-failure exercise or a procedure for deliberately entering adverse weather.

Use the applicable [FAA Airman Certification Standards catalog](https://www.faa.gov/training_testing/testing/acs) to identify current references with the instructor. Airman Certification Standards, or **ACS**, describe knowledge, risk-management and skill expectations for certification tasks. They can help make an assessment objective without turning your review into a verbatim replay of a practical test.

Ask for feedback on the process as well as the final result. You may arrive at the correct answer after losing track of another task. A debrief that identifies when that happened gives you a more precise training objective than a broad label such as “workload management.”

## Compare three fictional pilot profiles

The following are **invented preparation examples**, not complete review syllabuses, required task lists or findings about real pilots. Each instructor still determines the scope needed for that pilot, aircraft and operation.

| Fictional pilot | Starting point for the review discussion | Evidence to ask the instructor to assess |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A flies a familiar single-engine airplane often, almost entirely between the same nearby airports. They want to begin longer trips through busier airspace. | Bring a planned unfamiliar destination, its arrival information and realistic alternatives. Explain which airspace and radio tasks feel least familiar. | Can the pilot explain and manage the proposed route, changing instructions and destination choices while maintaining control and situational awareness? |
| B returns after a long gap and has booked an airplane with equipment they haven't used. They expect the review to restore their former confidence immediately. | Disclose the gap and equipment change in advance. Ask whether a staged refresher and equipment training should precede or accompany the review. | What training and assessment are needed before the instructor can establish satisfactory completion, and what separate checkout or operating limits need attention? |
| C flies a modern panel regularly but avoids hand flying except during takeoff and landing. They are considering a more demanding travel mission. | Identify the actual automation and the tasks that become difficult without it. Bring the aircraft's applicable normal and abnormal procedures. | Can the pilot manage the aircraft and workload when the expected automation is unavailable, within a safely planned instructor-led session? |

These pilots may need different allocations of ground discussion and flight training. The table doesn't tell an instructor to omit basic handling, aircraft knowledge or any other area necessary for safe operation. It helps the pilot supply information that makes those choices more informed.

## Leave with a specific debrief

Ask the instructor to distinguish what you demonstrated satisfactorily from what still needs practice. For each weak area, record the observed problem, the next training activity and the evidence that would show improvement. A written follow-up is easier to act on than a general intention to be more current.

The [FAA's post-review guidance](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-98E.pdf) explains that an unsatisfactory review is logged as instruction rather than a failed practical test, with further training recommended. The instructor gives the satisfactory-completion endorsement when appropriate. Time spent training can be productive even when it doesn't produce that endorsement on the first appointment.

Here is an **original follow-up format**. Suppose the review reveals that a pilot selects another airport promptly but doesn't organize the arrival information before reaching it. Record the next step as an instructor-led diversion session that assesses aircraft control, selection of the alternative and a timely arrival briefing. Don't replace that goal with a bare instruction to accumulate more hours.

Choose a date to review progress with the instructor. Agree what can be prepared on the ground, what needs dual instruction and what independent practice is appropriate for your qualifications and current proficiency. Avoid assigning yourself an unsupervised exercise merely because it appeared during the review.

## Make the follow-up fit your next airplane and mission

Revisit the preparation sheet when your flying changes, rather than waiting for another calendar deadline to notice a gap. A new destination, different panel or higher-performance aircraft can create training needs before the next review is due. The review is one opportunity to identify them, not a forecast of readiness for every future trip.

If you are shopping, include that training commitment in the shortlist and ownership budget.
