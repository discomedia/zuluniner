# Buying an airplane for IFR: how to check an avionics ad

Before paying more for an “IFR panel,” ask the seller to connect the equipment list to the airplane's approval documents, maintenance records and the procedures you intend to fly. A photograph can help you identify a navigator. It can't establish the installed system's approved capabilities or the status of its inspections.

Start with one flight you want the airplane to support. Then work backward from its navigation requirements, the aircraft documents and the evidence the seller can provide. That gives an avionics shop and your instructor a specific question to investigate before you commit to the purchase.

A [pilot's question about aircraft advertised as IFR certified](https://www.reddit.com/r/flying/comments/bzyuxo/) shows how easily the phrase mixes equipment, installation approval and inspection status. This US-first buying guide separates those checks for an ordinary Part 91 small piston airplane with a standard airworthiness certificate. Sources were checked on **21 October 2026**; the inspected eCFR pages display Title 14 as updated through 19 October.

## Write the IFR mission before reading the panel list

Instrument flight rules, or **IFR**, cover many possible flights. Write down your intended routes and representative destination and alternate procedures. Include the approach you expect to use when the weather deteriorates and what you would do if satellite navigation became unavailable.

For example, your personal requirement might be a GPS approach with vertical guidance and an autopilot that can follow it. Another buyer might accept hand flying and place more weight on conventional radio navigation. These are different purchase requirements even if both buyers describe the mission as cross-country IFR.

[§91.205(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.205) requires communication and navigation equipment suitable for the route, alongside the applicable day, night and instrument equipment. Its requirements include attitude and direction indications, an appropriate altimeter, time indication and electrical capacity. Use the actual provision and the airplane's approved configuration; a remembered equipment mnemonic won't establish whether a modified panel satisfies them.

Keep personal preferences visible on the same sheet. An autopilot may be essential to your willingness to make a particular trip even when the applicable operating rules don't make that specific capability a requirement. Price the airplane against the mission you will actually accept.

## Start with the aircraft and installation documents

Ask for the current approved flight-manual material, applicable supplements, equipment list and installation records for the advertised configuration. Identify the airframe by registration and serial number. Have the shop reconcile those records with the installed units, rather than assuming every example of the model has the same approval.

[§91.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.9) requires compliance with operating limitations in the approved manual, markings and placards. Its aircraft-document requirements are another reason to ask for the actual material that accompanies the sale. An impressive equipment inventory doesn't answer an unresolved operating limitation.

An **airplane flight manual supplement**, or **AFMS**, can describe the installation's capabilities and restrictions. A [**supplemental type certificate**](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/air_cert/design_approvals/stc), or **STC**, is an approval for a change to the type design; ask which approval supports the alteration and which associated documents apply. Have a qualified shop establish the paperwork needed for this particular installation, including any applicable alteration records and continued-maintenance instructions.

For a concrete reference, Garmin's [GTN 6XX/7XX AFMS, 190-01007-A2 revision 9](https://static.garmin.com/pumac/190-01007-a2_09.pdf), dated 3 January 2020, addresses external guidance indicators and installation-dependent annunciation in section 2.3. Section 2.13 includes an installer-selectable restriction on vertical autopilot coupling. Those are details a navigator's model name leaves unresolved.

That is a dated manufacturer example for the earlier GTN family, not confirmation of today's revision or applicability to a candidate, and not a GTN Xi operating reference. Obtain the current approved supplement for the actual installation. A generic downloaded copy can't fill in its configuration selections.

## Match the navigation equipment to the procedures

For each representative procedure, identify what supplies the required navigation information and where the pilot sees it. An instrument landing system (**ILS**) uses different equipment from a GPS-based approach. A panel with a moving map doesn't tell you which sources are installed, approved and functioning.

The [FAA Aeronautical Information Manual's GPS guidance](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap1_section_1.html) ties IFR GPS use to equipment and installation approval and the applicable flight-manual material. Don't infer approach approval from a receiver being mounted in the panel. Ask the shop to identify the approved navigation functions, rather than relying on the seller's shorthand.

An **RNAV (GPS)** approach is an area-navigation procedure using GPS. **Localizer performance with vertical guidance (LPV)** is a particular line of approach minima with lateral and vertical guidance supported by an eligible installation and available service. Ask which published minima the installation can support, then check the procedure, current information and system status for the intended flight. Neither label promises that every destination has a suitable approach.

Build a short capability matrix with your instructor: planned departure, en route navigation, destination approach, alternate and a credible fallback. Record a document reference for each claimed capability. If a row depends on equipment you would install after purchase, obtain a written scope and quote before treating that row as satisfied.

## Read the inspection entries separately

Ask the seller what they mean by “IFR checks current” and request the dated entries. The relevant rules cover different systems and circumstances. A recent annual-inspection entry doesn't replace the evidence for these separate requirements.

| Check | What to establish from the records |
| --- | --- |
| Altimeter, static pressure and altitude reporting | Under [§91.411](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.411), the applicable tests for controlled-airspace IFR are required within the preceding 24 calendar months. Check the systems covered and the tested altitude. |
| Transponder | [§91.413](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.413) has its own 24-calendar-month test requirement for the transponders it specifies. Identify the unit and the entry rather than treating the static-system date as proof. |
| VOR navigation | [§91.171](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.171) applies when operating IFR using the VOR system. It provides an approved-procedure route or an operational check within the preceding 30 days, with specified limits and records. |
| Work since the last test | Ask whether intervening maintenance triggered another test. A date inside a recurring interval doesn't resolve every post-maintenance requirement. |

Here is an **original hypothetical records exercise**. On 21 October 2026, a static-system test from October 2024 and a transponder test from September 2024 have different calendar-month positions. The latter is beyond the preceding 24-calendar-month interval. Don't copy the newer-looking date onto both systems.

Now suppose the static system was opened and closed during work this month. Section 91.411 includes a subsequent test requirement, with stated exceptions for drain and alternate-static valve use. It also addresses certain altitude-reporting maintenance. Have the maintainer determine which entries that work required; the older recurring test alone doesn't answer the new question.

Inoperative equipment needs its own assessment under [§91.213](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.213) and the applicable aircraft documents. A placard on a failed instrument doesn't by itself establish permission for your proposed IFR flight. Ask the shop to resolve defects before assigning the airplane a dispatch capability.

## Check the airborne database and its update arrangements

Ask the seller to show the navigation database's effective dates in the installed navigator. Separate that database from charts on a tablet and from any terrain, obstacle or display databases. A subscription receipt isn't proof that the intended data were loaded into the correct unit.

The [FAA's GPS approach guidance](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap1_section_1.html) requires the procedure to be retrievable by name from the current airborne navigation database; manually building an approach from a series of waypoints doesn't satisfy that instruction. En route use and other database questions need the guidance and limitations applicable to the equipment and operation. Don't apply an expired-database answer from a different navigator to the one you are buying.

Request a handover demonstration of the update process, required media or hardware, account arrangements and database coverage. Obtain current supplier terms for transfer and renewal. No subscription price or transfer entitlement is assumed here.

Write down who will resolve an unavailable procedure or data discrepancy. A convenient update process and a known support route can affect ownership even when the hardware meets your specification. Include the recurring costs in your [airplane ownership budget](/blog/airplane-ownership-cost-50-100-200-hours).

## Trace the guidance through the displays and autopilot

Ask the shop and instructor to explain the installed navigation-source selection, course-deviation display and autopilot interfaces. A **course deviation indicator**, or **CDI**, shows deviation from a selected course; a **horizontal situation indicator**, or **HSI**, combines course information with heading presentation. The exact installation determines which display and annunciations the pilot must use.

Have them distinguish what the navigator can calculate, what the display can show, and what the autopilot is approved and configured to follow. The Garmin reference above illustrates why an installation can have a coupling limitation even when the navigator provides guidance. Identify the actual autopilot and its supplement before assigning it an approach capability.

Ask for evidence of how the system behaves during source changes, an approach, the missed approach and a loss of an expected input. These are topics for an appropriate shop evaluation and instructor-led checkout, not a request for an unqualified buyer to experiment in instrument conditions. Record any discrepancy and the proposed remedy.

Also ask how the installed flight instruments are powered and which indications survive the failures addressed in the aircraft's procedures. Two screens alone don't establish independent power or independent sensing. Use the applicable system documentation to build the failure picture before choosing your training and personal limits.

## Compare three fictional avionics ads

These are **invented buying cases**, not descriptions or findings for aircraft in ZuluNiner's listings. Each isolates a different unanswered question; none establishes that an airplane is airworthy or ready for a flight.

| Fictional ad and evidence | What changes the buying decision? |
| --- | --- |
| A: “Modern GPS, two-axis autopilot, IFR ready.” The seller supplies a panel photo but no applicable AFMS; the supplied transponder test is from September 2024. | Resolve the installation approval and missing limitations, and price the overdue test and any defects found. The equipment photograph leaves both documentation and maintenance work open. |
| B: “Current IFR inspections, traditional instruments.” The documented navigator is restricted to VFR use; the buyer's mission calls for RNAV (GPS) approaches. | The inspection status doesn't create the missing navigation capability. Establish an approved upgrade scope and downtime, or choose a candidate that already supports the mission. |
| C: Approved GPS capability is documented, but the applicable autopilot material permits only lateral coupling on the proposed GPS approach. The buyer wants coupled vertical guidance. | Decide with the instructor whether the existing capability fits the intended operation and personal policy. Obtain an approved modification proposal before assuming that a setting or database update will add vertical coupling. |

These cases can produce different purchase decisions without ranking glass displays above round instruments. Your comparison needs verified capability, the cost of closing gaps and the training commitment. Keep the [prebuy evaluation](/blog/prebuy-vs-annual-inspection) broad enough to cover the rest of the airplane too.

## Turn the missing evidence into a purchase decision

[Download the IFR avionics seller-question worksheet](/blog/ifr-avionics-ad-checklist-worksheet.md). It gives you a place to record the intended procedures, exact unit identities, document references, inspection entries and unresolved work. Send a focused records request before spending money on a distant inspection.

Use the replies to commission a written avionics evaluation. Agree which records and functions the shop will assess, how discrepancies will be reported, and whether proposed costs are estimates or firm quotations. No aircraft was inspected and no shop quote or independent professional review was obtained for this article.

The pilot's qualifications, recency and proficiency also need a separate check. A purchase and a successful equipment evaluation don't supply an instrument rating or a checkout. If the airplane is also intended for initial training, resolve the instructor and insurance arrangement described in our [buy-for-training guide](/blog/buy-airplane-private-pilot-training).

Then [browse available aircraft](/aircraft) against your written mission. Shortlist a candidate when its documented capability fits, or when you have an acceptable, priced plan for the missing work. Leave an unresolved panel claim as an open item in the purchase decision.
