# Low engine hours, old overhaul: bargain or liability?

An engine with 400 hours since overhaul can be a good purchase, an expensive uncertainty, or an engine that needs major work before you fly it. The hour figure alone can't tell you which. Start with the overhaul date, the work actually performed, and what happened between that date and today.

For a US buyer, ask whether the engine can legally and safely stay in service, and whether you can fund an early replacement. A maintenance assessment addresses the first; a purchase budget addresses the second.

## Read the event behind the abbreviation

Aircraft ads compress a long maintenance history into a few letters. Expand them before comparing prices:

| Ad term | What it usually describes | What to request |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SMOH or TSMOH | Hours since major overhaul; time since major overhaul | Signed overhaul entry, date, shop, work order and parts documentation |
| STOH | Hours since top overhaul, usually work on cylinders and associated parts | Exact cylinders and parts repaired or replaced; the engine's separate SMOH |
| SFRM | Hours since factory remanufacture | Factory paperwork establishing the actual maintenance event and recording basis |
| SNEW or TTSN | Hours since new or total time since new | Engine identity, delivery/installation records and subsequent time continuity |

Lycoming's [discussion of low-time engines](https://www.lycoming.com/content/low-time-engine-may-not-mean-quality-and-value) identifies these abbreviations and cautions that overhaul quality affects the prospect of reaching the published interval. Sellers don't all use abbreviations consistently.

The FAA gives **overhauled** and **rebuilt** specific meanings in maintenance records. An overhaul requires disassembly, cleaning, inspection, necessary repair, reassembly and testing to the specified standards. Rebuilding requires testing and conformity to the tolerances and limits of a new item, with qualifying new or used parts. Neither word means that every component is new. [14 CFR §43.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/section-43.2)

A top overhaul doesn't establish that the crankcase was split or the crankshaft, bearings and camshaft (which operates the valves) underwent a complete engine overhaul. If an ad says “150 STOH,” ask for the engine's total time and SMOH. Recent cylinder work can coexist with a much older engine assembly; price the documented scope rather than treating the top-overhaul hours as the whole engine's age.

“Zero time” also needs paperwork. [§91.421](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.421) allows a new maintenance record without prior operating history for an engine rebuilt by the manufacturer or an agency it approves. A field overhaul showing zero SMOH doesn't automatically grant that recording status.

Ask why each maintenance event occurred. Scheduled work, corrosion, persistent oil consumption and a propeller strike raise different follow-up questions. FAA [maintenance-record guidance, AC 43-9D](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_43-9D_Ed_Upd.pdf), also warns that terms such as “remanufactured” or “reconditioned” don't by themselves prove the work required for a regulatory rebuild.

## Hours and calendar age describe different exposure

Operating hours record accumulated use. Calendar age includes every month the engine spends parked. Neither replaces the other.

Lycoming's [preservation guidance, Service Letter L180B](https://www.lycoming.com/content/service-letter-no-l180b), explains that moisture and combustion products can attack cylinder walls and bearing surfaces during inactivity. Humidity and storage conditions affect the need for preservation. A hangar tells you where the aircraft lived; preservation records tell you what someone did to protect the engine.

“Flown every year” leaves plenty unresolved. Was that regular cross-country flying or a few hours immediately before each annual? Did the aircraft spend several seasons inactive? Did an owner preserve it during an extended absence and follow the required return-to-service process?

A lifetime average conceals those gaps. An engine with 400 hours accumulated over 16 years averages 25 hours a year. It could have flown 25 hours each year, or accumulated most of its time in the first four years and spent the rest largely parked. Your next question should concern the calendar between log entries.

Be cautious about “started every month.” Lycoming's [explanation of infrequent operation](https://www.lycoming.com/content/frequency-flight-and-its-affect-engine) warns that ground running intended to prevent corrosion can instead leave water and acids in the oil when the engine doesn't reach appropriate operating conditions. Don't invent a ground-running routine to compensate for missing preservation; ask the maintainer to assess the documented history against the applicable instructions.

More frequent flight doesn't guarantee condition. Identify exposure and uncertainty, then decide what inspection or budget response each deserves.

## Check the current TBO for the actual engine

**Time between overhaul (TBO)** is an interval published for a specified engine and qualifying conditions. It isn't a balance of guaranteed hours that transfers to the buyer.

Recent revisions make generic figures particularly risky. Lycoming's [Service Instruction 1009BF, dated 20 July 2026](https://www.lycoming.com/sites/default/files/file/2026-07/SI1009BF_%20TBO_Schedule.pdf), supersedes 1009BE. Table 1 now separates factory-new/rebuilt engines from overhauled engines: the IO-360-L2A row lists 2,600 hours in the former column and 2,000 in the latter, with applicable overhaul notes. The factory column covers the first overhaul cycle following factory delivery.

The instruction also specifies a 12-year calendar interval and sets conditions for exceeding it in the identified operations, including examination by appropriately qualified maintenance personnel, repeated annually or as necessary, and concurrence from the controlling aviation authority. It excludes engines affected by, and not compliant with, airworthiness directive (AD) 2012-19-01. Have the maintainer read the full instruction and its notes against the engine's records; the model name alone doesn't establish eligibility.

For Continental AvGas engines, use the applicable engine manuals and current **M-0 Standard Practice Maintenance Manual**. Continental's [official publication distributor lists M-0 with a publication date of 27 July 2026](https://continental.gilmoreglobal.com/en/product/1a045570-e77e-4988-9989-c4344c4a86d5). Ask the inspecting shop to identify the revision and model-specific interval it applied. Continental explains access to these manuals on its [technical-publications page](https://continental.aero/technical-publications/).

### Recommendation, requirement and airworthiness

For US aircraft, establish what makes an interval applicable:

* **Airworthiness directives (ADs):** operating a product that doesn't meet an applicable AD violates [§39.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-39/section-39.7). Check the engine and installed components, including relevant serial numbers and recurring actions.
* **Airworthiness limitations:** [§91.403(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.403) addresses mandatory replacement times, inspection intervals and related procedures in an airworthiness limitations section.
* **The operation and maintenance program:** identify the operating rules and selected or approved inspection/maintenance program. The requirements differ; [§91.409](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.409) describes several inspection arrangements. Don't transfer advice about one private aircraft to a different operation.

Manufacturer instructions and recommendations still inform the maintenance assessment. A label such as “recommended” doesn't settle every regulatory or program question, and the word “mandatory” on a manufacturer's document doesn't answer it by itself. Ask the shop to explain the applicable basis in writing if exceeding an interval is part of the purchase proposal.

The owner or operator has the primary responsibility for maintaining airworthiness under [§91.403(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.403). An engine below its published TBO can still have an airworthiness defect. Available hours never justify overlooking a finding.

## Build a timeline before booking an inspection

Request readable scans of the engine records before paying for travel. Match the model and serial number in those records to the installed engine during the inspection.

Make a timeline of dates, recorded time, work performed and unresolved questions. Include preservation and return to service alongside overhaul, repairs and abnormal events. Note time-measuring instrument replacements that affect the readings.

FAA [AC 43-9D](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_43-9D_Ed_Upd.pdf) discusses time-record continuity, recording-device changes and missing records. It distinguishes a rebuilt engine's permitted new history from gaps that need reconstruction. For a buyer, an unexplained gap is a question to resolve with the seller and inspecting shop; it isn't evidence that nothing happened.

Three requests often clarify an apparently low-time engine:

1. **The overhaul package:** who performed the work, when, which parts were replaced, what testing occurred, and what accessories were included?
2. **The recent utilization history:** what do dated records establish about use and inactivity, particularly during the last few years?
3. **The inspection evidence:** can the seller supply reports, photographs and trends, rather than only “compressions good”?

Agree on records review and spending checkpoints with an independent shop experienced in that engine. Our [prebuy-versus-annual guide](/blog/prebuy-vs-annual-inspection) explains inspection scope and handling findings.

## What the tests can establish

Commission an engine assessment that combines evidence. Ask the mechanic to explain each finding, the follow-up required and what couldn't be inspected.

| Evidence | What it contributes | What it doesn't establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Differential compression | Cylinder sealing information under the test conditions | Complete condition of the crankshaft, bearings or camshaft |
| Cylinder borescope photographs | Views of accessible cylinder surfaces and valve areas | Every internal engine surface or a guaranteed service life |
| Filter/screen inspection | Evidence of material caught in the oil system | Proof that all internal components are undamaged |
| Laboratory oil analysis | Wear-metal information, especially across comparable samples | A complete diagnosis from one sample |
| Operating data | Recorded behavior and changes under identifiable conditions | Mechanical clearance measurements or a teardown inspection |

Lycoming's [cylinder-compression instruction](https://www.lycoming.com/sites/default/files/attachments/Cylinder%2520Compression.pdf) describes a static leak-rate test and stresses the tester's judgment. A seller's readings need the engine identity, test method, conditions and interpretation beside them. Avoid importing a threshold from another manufacturer or engine procedure.

A borescope is a small optical inspection instrument. Photographs preserve a baseline; ask what they show and which areas remain inaccessible. A clean cylinder view doesn't clear the rest of the engine. Lycoming's [LIO-360-B1G6 maintenance manual](https://www.lycoming.com/sites/default/files/attachments/MM-LIO-360-B1G6.pdf), for example, describes cylinder-wall and valve-area inspections separately. Have the shop apply the current instructions for your engine.

Oil analysis and filter inspection answer different questions. The FAA's [Powerplant Handbook, Chapter 8](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/10_amtp_ch8.pdf), describes microscopic wear-metal analysis alongside inspection for particles in the oil system. Lycoming's [oil-analysis explanation](https://www.lycoming.com/content/why-oil-analysis-important) emphasizes repeated samples to establish an individual engine's pattern. Request the sample dates, hours on each oil interval and recent maintenance history so the shop can interpret the reports.

An engine monitor can preserve temperature and operating information. Ask for downloadable records if available; a photograph of a panel during one flight offers a smaller window. FAA [AC 20-105C, with its July 2024 editorial update](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_20-105C_Ed_Upd_%287-2-24%29.pdf) supports trend monitoring across inspections and operating observations, with readings assessed against manufacturer guidance. Instrument accuracy and operating context belong in that assessment.

These checks reduce uncertainty without removing it. If inactivity or findings raise concern about components the initial inspection can't reach, ask the shop what further inspection would establish, how intrusive it is, and its likely cost before authorizing it. Don't turn a reassuring test into a prediction of remaining hours.

## Two hypothetical ads

The following histories are invented to demonstrate comparison. They describe no ZuluNiner listing and imply no inspection result.

| Detail | Aircraft A | Aircraft B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advertised engine time | 400 SMOH | 1,100 SMOH |
| Overhaul date | 9 October 2010 | 9 October 2018 |
| Calendar age on 9 October 2026 | 16 years | 8 years |
| Average annual use since overhaul | 25 hours | 137.5 hours |
| Recent history | Most time early; extended later inactivity | Records indicate distributed use through recent years |
| Preservation | Seller hasn't supplied records | Documented during a lengthy interruption |
| Supporting evidence | Recent compression figures only | Repeated oil reports, filter findings and dated cylinder photographs |

Aircraft A's lower SMOH leaves more to investigate. Its calendar age warrants specific review of manufacturer guidance and applicable requirements. Missing preservation and internal-condition evidence also make it difficult to justify a price premium for the low hours.

Aircraft B gives the inspector more history to assess. It may still need expensive work, and its higher hours need evaluation against the correct published interval and findings. The table doesn't rank either engine as airworthy.

As a buyer, you might continue investigating B first if your priority is a documented recent operating history. A could still suit a purchase that anticipates engine work, provided the airframe suits the mission, the inspection establishes the required actions and the price leaves the necessary funds available.

Before either inspection, check that the aircraft carries your intended passengers and trip fuel. An engine budget won't solve a loading mismatch; the [four-seat payload guide](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) provides that earlier screen.

## Budget for timing uncertainty

Separate a future engine reserve from money available immediately after purchase. An hourly reserve accumulates only when you fly and fund it; an unexpected first-year engine bill can arrive before that account grows.

For a hypothetical calculation, assume an **installed engine-work budget of USD 45,000**, no existing reserve and **1,200 planned flying hours** over which to fund it. These are teaching assumptions, not a market quote or a remaining-life estimate:

**USD 45,000 ÷ 1,200 hours = USD 37.50 per hour.**

At 75 hours a year, you'd contribute **USD 2,812.50 annually**. After two years, the reserve would hold **USD 5,625**, leaving **USD 39,375** unfunded if that assumed bill arrived then. The calculation excludes interest, inflation and any other withdrawals.

Get a dated written quote for the particular engine and installation. Ask about removal and installation labor, shipping, core acceptance, accessories, hoses, oil-system work, taxes and exclusions. Ask how findings can change the estimate and what downtime to expect. Compare the whole project rather than an advertised overhaul price alone.

Keep the purchase-price comparison separate from the airworthiness decision. A discount can help finance required work. It can't make an unresolved defect acceptable to fly.

## A buyer's decision worksheet

Complete this with the seller's documents and your inspecting shop. Write “unknown” where evidence is missing.

| Question | Record your answer |
| --- | --- |
| Does engine identity match the records? | Model, serial number and time-record basis |
| What event started the advertised hours? | Overhaul, rebuild or limited repair; date and supporting entry |
| Which interval applies? | Manufacturer document/revision, exact model, column, notes and qualifying history |
| What requirements control continued operation? | Applicable ADs, airworthiness limitations and operating/maintenance program |
| What happened during inactivity? | Dates, environment, preservation and return-to-service records |
| What does the current assessment establish? | Findings, required action and inaccessible areas |
| What remains unresolved? | Additional inspection or documentation, cost and decision deadline |
| Can you fund early engine work? | Current installed quote, immediately available funds and downtime tolerance |

If the records and inspection support continued service, compare price with the remaining uncertainty and your ownership budget. If further evidence is needed, make that investigation a purchase condition. If you can't fund the work the assessment calls for, another aircraft may fit your plans better.

When you [browse aircraft for sale](/aircraft), read the engine's date and history alongside its hours. Request the records that explain the number before deciding how much that number is worth.
