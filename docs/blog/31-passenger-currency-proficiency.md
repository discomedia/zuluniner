# You're legal to carry passengers—but ready for this flight?

Passenger currency establishes recent experience for a particular operation. Readiness also depends on what you've practiced, the airplane you'll fly and the conditions you'll face. A logbook can establish the first without answering the second.

Treat the decision as two checks: verify the applicable requirements from your records, then compare your recent performance with the proposed trip. A current pilot may still need instruction, a simpler itinerary or a different way to travel.

A pilot asking about a [first night flight with passengers](https://www.reddit.com/r/flying/comments/1b2dk0u/first_time_taking_passengers_up_at_night/) described being current but wanting to prepare for the unfamiliar passenger task. That is the question here. This US-first guide addresses ordinary private-pilot airplane operations under Part 91; specialized exceptions and commercial operations need their own review.

Rules were checked on **31 October 2026** against eCFR pages displaying Title 14 current through 29 October. The scenario cards below are original fictional examples, not reports of flights or instructor endorsements.

## Keep the separate checks visible

Start a trip note with separate rows for each requirement. Record the evidence and the next date or operating change that could affect it. Avoid a single “current” flag that hides which question you actually answered.

| Check | Evidence to examine | What it doesn't establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flight review or qualifying alternative | Dated record and applicable endorsement or qualifying event | Passenger landing recency |
| General passenger recency | Relevant takeoffs and landings, dates and aircraft | Night passenger recency by itself |
| Night passenger recency | Full-stop events in the required time window | Readiness for a dark unfamiliar arrival |
| Instrument recency, when applicable | Qualifying instrument tasks and dates, or a check | Passenger landing recency or mission proficiency |
| Readiness for this trip | Recent comparable performance, limitations and training needs | Satisfaction of a missing legal requirement |

For the usual flight-review route, [§61.56(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-61/subpart-A/section-61.56) uses a 24-calendar-month period and requires satisfactory completion in an aircraft for which you're rated, with the authorized instructor's endorsement. The section also provides qualifying alternatives and exceptions. Identify the provision supported by your records rather than assuming every instructional flight resets the review period.

The [flight-review preparation guide](/blog/flight-review-preparation) covers arranging that review. Here, the important recordkeeping step is to keep its result separate from the other rows. Completing one event may produce evidence for several requirements, but each row still needs its own supporting facts.

Other applicable pilot qualifications, aircraft requirements and operating rules also need checking. This recency worksheet isn't an all-purpose dispatch approval.

## Read the passenger-recency entries

For the ordinary case, [§61.57(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-61/subpart-A/section-61.57) requires three takeoffs and three landings within the preceding 90 days, as sole manipulator of the controls, in the same category, class and type where those ratings are required. For a tailwheel airplane, the qualifying landings must be full stops in a tailwheel airplane. The section contains exceptions and specific device provisions.

Read the actual entries rather than the total-hours column. A long cruise flight and a session with repeated landings can add similar flight time while producing different evidence for this check. An entry saying only “dual” or “night” may leave you needing more detail about what occurred.

Aircraft category and class don't mean make and model. Moving between two single-engine land airplanes can leave the recency row unchanged while creating a substantial training need. A different cockpit, landing picture or set of systems can change what you'll have to manage on the passenger flight.

For a proposed operation near a date boundary, count from the actual flight date and the qualifying events. Ninety days is a rolling period; it isn't three calendar months. Recheck the return flight if it falls on a different date, rather than carrying forward the outbound decision automatically.

## Use the right night clock

Under the ordinary [§61.57(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-61/subpart-A/section-61.57) night passenger rule, the relevant operating period starts one hour after sunset and ends one hour before sunrise. The required three takeoffs and three full-stop landings must have occurred within that same kind of period during the preceding 90 days, with the specified control and aircraft qualifications.

The general definition of night in [§1.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/section-1.1) uses the end of evening civil twilight and beginning of morning civil twilight. A logbook's night-time total therefore doesn't, on its own, establish the timing of qualifying passenger-recency events.

For an **invented clock example**, assume sunset at 18:00 and the end of evening civil twilight at 18:30. Takeoffs and landings between 18:40 and 18:55 occur after the assumed twilight boundary but before the 19:00 passenger-rule boundary. These invented times aren't solar data for an actual airport or date.

Record the local date, location and time basis when checking real events. For a planned return near a boundary, include a delay in the itinerary assessment. Don't build the plan around arriving at the last possible minute before a different requirement applies.

## Check instrument recency separately

An instrument rating and recent instrument experience are separate items. For the airplane case, [§61.57(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-61/subpart-A/section-61.57) specifies six approaches, holding procedures/tasks, and interception/tracking in the six calendar months preceding the month of the flight, under qualifying actual or simulated conditions. Check the applicable aircraft or approved-device provisions and what was performed and logged.

If that experience has lapsed, investigate the permitted recovery route before planning to act as pilot in command under instrument flight rules (IFR). The later period for regaining experience isn't permission to fly as an uncurrent IFR pilot in command. After more than six calendar months of failing to meet those experience requirements, §61.57(d) requires an instrument proficiency check (IPC), subject to the section's exceptions.

The FAA's [instrument-currency clarification](https://www.faasafety.gov/SPANS/noticeView.aspx?nid=3489) illustrates that recovery distinction. It is a historical 2012 notice, checked here against the current regulation; use the current rule for your records and circumstances.

Neither a new IPC nor a passenger-recency session should be assumed to settle every other row. If an event is intended to satisfy more than one requirement, arrange that scope beforehand and confirm what was actually completed and recorded.

## Work through six scenario cards

These are **fictional ground-study cases**. Unless a card identifies a gap, assume the other applicable legal requirements have been met. The planning responses show how to identify the missing evidence; they don't certify a pilot or approve a flight.

### Card 1: A review with one landing

A pilot completed a satisfactory flight review last week. It included one takeoff and landing; there are no other qualifying events in the relevant passenger-recency period. They propose a daytime flight with a friend.

The review record supports its own row. The stated landing record leaves the passenger-recency row incomplete. The response is to resolve that gap before taking the friend, rather than treating the review endorsement as a blanket currency reset.

### Card 2: The right label, uncertain times

A pilot has three recent full-stop landings entered as “night,” but the entries don't establish whether the associated takeoffs and landings occurred in the passenger-rule window. A late return with a passenger is proposed.

The label alone doesn't answer the timing question. Verify the actual events and applicable solar times. If the evidence doesn't establish the requirement, change the plan or arrange appropriate training; don't fill the gap with an assumed departure time.

### Card 3: A new airplane within the class

A pilot is passenger-current in a familiar single-engine land trainer. The proposed flight uses a different single-engine land airplane with unfamiliar avionics and a substantially different landing picture. No type rating is required for either airplane in this invented case.

The class-based recency question and the transition-training question produce different answers. Arrange training that addresses the actual installation and handling before using the first passenger trip to discover the gaps. Also check any separate qualifications and operating limitations that the particular aircraft requires.

### Card 4: A day trip grows into an IFR return

A pilot has current passenger landing experience and an instrument rating, but their instrument records don't support current IFR privileges. The original itinerary was visual flight rules (VFR); the return plan changes to IFR.

The itinerary change creates an unresolved instrument-recency issue. Replan around privileges that are actually supported, or complete the appropriate recovery process before the proposed operation. An IFR-equipped airplane and an available clearance don't supply missing pilot experience.

### Card 5: Current, with a narrow recent pattern

A pilot has met the relevant legal requirements but has recently flown only daylight circuits at a familiar airport. The proposed passenger trip ends at an unfamiliar airport after dark.

The records support the stated currency; they provide little evidence of recent performance on the proposed arrival task. Discuss the gap with an instructor and consider a simpler daylight itinerary. The outcome to seek is a specific training or trip change, not an invented universal number of hours that makes everyone ready.

### Card 6: The calendar shortcut

For an invented flight on 31 October 2026, a pilot's only otherwise qualifying passenger-recency session was on 1 August 2026. That was 91 days earlier. They described it as “within the last three months.”

The day count exposes the faulty shortcut. Check the actual qualifying dates against the rule rather than substituting month names. The separate flight-review calendar calculation doesn't change that passenger-recency result.

## Describe proficiency with evidence

Replace “I feel comfortable” with a short account of what you've recently done: the airplane and avionics, conditions, tasks, assistance needed and unfinished training items. This gives an instructor something concrete to assess.

The FAA's active [AC 61-98E](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-98E.pdf) encourages proficiency training beyond minimum currency. It also addresses aircraft-specific automation knowledge and the ability to operate when automation fails. A session that only adds landings may leave those tasks untouched.

Bring the proposed trip into the discussion. For example, “I've recently flown only local daytime circuits; I haven't practiced this navigator's arrival changes or flown to this destination after dark.” That original briefing example identifies training questions without claiming a flight instructor has answered them.

The FAA's [personal-minimums guidance](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-01/Personal%20Minimums.pdf) ties margins to the skills and aircraft capability a particular flight requires, compared with those available through training and experience. Use that comparison to select a training task or simplify the flight. Avoid turning someone else's ceiling, wind or recency limit into an automatic personal standard.

## Make a plan for the actual passenger trip

Use the [passenger currency and readiness scenario cards](/blog/passenger-currency-readiness-cards.md) to separate the record check from the trip decision. The downloadable companion collects the six cases and a blank worksheet for your own operation.

Before committing to a departure, write down the intended route and timing, the return plan, unresolved record questions and the conditions that require a change. Add a practical alternative for passengers if flying doesn't work. A restaurant reservation or a waiting friend should be part of the pressure assessment, not evidence of readiness.

Keep the [personal-minimums guide](/blog/vfr-personal-minimums) beside the currency record, and use the [route-weather case](/blog/destination-metar-route-weather) when examining the actual itinerary. The date check answers one part of the decision; the flight still needs its own planning and current information.

## Choose an airplane you can keep practicing in

When browsing [available aircraft](/aircraft), include access to suitable instruction and the cost of continuing practice in the ownership plan. Describe the passenger trips you expect to make, the equipment you want to use and the schedule flexibility you have.

Ask about the actual aircraft's configuration and training support. Then test whether your time and budget can support maintaining the skills that configuration and mission demand. Buying more equipment creates no logbook event and supplies no evidence that you've practiced using it.
