# Cessna 172/180 conversions: what the extra horsepower doesn't tell you

A Cessna 172 advertised with 134 kW (180 hp) still needs a configuration check. The engine rating doesn't establish its approved loaded weight, passenger payload, propeller installation or departure performance. Two airplanes described as “172/180” can leave you with different operating documents and different answers to your mission.

Start with the exact base model and serial number, then identify the installed approvals. Ask for the records before comparing the conversion's advertised benefits with another 172 or a 182. Otherwise, you can spend a buying trip discovering that the airplane you imagined isn't the one being sold.

This guide covers US aircraft and pilot rules. Sources were checked on **14 October 2026**; the eCFR pages displayed Title 14 as current through **9 October 2026**. The loading comparison below is entirely hypothetical. No individual aircraft's installation, airworthiness, insurance coverage or performance is verified here.

## Identify what the conversion actually changed

A **supplemental type certificate**, or **STC**, is FAA approval for a modification to an aeronautical product's original design. The [FAA's STC explanation](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/air_cert/design_approvals/stc) says the approval also addresses how the modification affects that original design. The identifying number is the route into the documents behind the change.

For a candidate airplane, record its model suffix, serial number, engine model and installed STC numbers. “Lycoming O-360” is a starting description; the suffix and approved installation matter. Keep the seller's description beside the supporting document rather than combining both into a presumed specification.

Air Plains' [current 172 upgrade page](https://www.airplains.com/cessna172-180hp) describes an O-360-A4M installation with a fixed-pitch propeller. Its [172R comparison](https://www.airplains.com/post/_172r) describes a different route using the IO-360-L2A engine and a propeller allowing 2,700 rpm. That page compares SA4428SW and SA10406SC, with differences in the alternate-air installation and equipment changes.

Those examples show why the ad's power number isn't enough. An earlier airplane with a replaced engine and a later airplane with a changed propeller can arrive at the same rating through different installations. Ask which configuration is present, when it was installed and which documents describe it now.

## Separate engine approval from higher-weight approval

For Air Plains conversions, the [manufacturer's FAQ](https://www.airplains.com/faq) identifies **SA4428SW** as the engine upgrade and **SA2196CE** as the gross-weight increase. It ties the reduced flap limit to the higher-weight approval, rather than to the engine upgrade alone. Don't assume that finding one STC number establishes the other installation.

| Document question | What it establishes | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which engine installation is approved? | The specified engine/configuration under the applicable approval | Installation records, equipment and applicable supplement |
| Is a higher loaded weight approved and installed? | The separate weight-change basis, where applicable | Model/serial applicability and required changes |
| Which limits now apply? | The airplane's current manual material and placards | Consistency with its installed configuration |
| What does it weigh empty today? | The current aircraft-specific weight record | Equipment changes after that record's date |

Air Plains' [published weight-increase options](https://www.airplains.com/post/useful-load) vary by model. For its listed conversions, the advertised increases include 91 kg (200 lb) for D–L models, 113 kg (250 lb) for M/N, 68 kg (150 lb) for P and 45 kg (100 lb) for R. These are descriptions of approval options, not a new weight limit you can assign to an airplane from its model name.

That same manufacturer page identifies required changes, including tailcone reinforcement where needed, flap limits, markings and tires. Have the inspecting mechanic trace the applicable requirements into the installation records and aircraft. A matching number in an ad leaves that work unfinished.

## Match the supplement to the airplane

One public example is Air Plains [document 172051](https://yokota374fss.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/airplane_flight_manual-172051.pdf), a supplement for 172D–H serials 17249545–17256512. Its cover describes SA2196CE together with SA4428SW, with a maximum normal-category weight of **1,134 kg (2,500 lb)**. The copy has revision pages dated 3 February 2012 and a 30° flap limit.

That reference copy illustrates applicability; it doesn't establish the current revision or installation on a candidate airplane. Use the aircraft's own documents, and request current material through the [manufacturer's document portal](https://www.airplains.com/file-share) when needed. Don't copy this supplement's limit into a 172M, N, P or R loading sheet.

Under [14 CFR §91.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.9), operation must comply with the applicable approved manual limitations, markings and placards. The rule also sets requirements for available manual material in US-registered aircraft. Reading the basic handbook and the installed supplements together is part of identifying the airplane's current limits.

Before a checkout, make a short list of changes to find in those documents: weight limits, loading envelope, flap range, operating speeds, engine/propeller limits and procedures. Ask the instructor to work through the changes with you. Keep unresolved conflicts on the list until the appropriate records or approval holder settle them.

## Calculate the payload change

The [FAA's weight-and-balance chapter](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak/12_phak_ch10.pdf) explains how installed equipment and alterations affect aircraft weight and balance. A higher permitted loaded weight and a heavier empty airplane act in opposite directions when calculating the allowance for people, fuel and bags.

For screening, use the applicable maximum loaded weight minus the documented basic empty weight. Then subtract usable fuel and any other load excluded from that empty-weight basis. Check the record's definitions so oil or unusable fuel isn't counted twice.

Here is an **invented teaching comparison**, not data for any Cessna model or STC. Assume the maximum ramp, takeoff and landing weights are equal within each case. Each basic empty weight includes full oil and unusable fuel. All three cases use **140 L (37.0 US gal)** of usable fuel at an assumed **0.72 kg/L**.

| Fictional case | Maximum loaded weight | Basic empty weight | Allowance before usable fuel | People-and-bags allowance with the assumed fuel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before alteration | 1,040 kg (2,293 lb) | 650 kg (1,433 lb) | 390 kg (860 lb) | 289.2 kg (638 lb) |
| Engine change; same weight limit | 1,040 kg (2,293 lb) | 675 kg (1,488 lb) | 365 kg (805 lb) | 264.2 kg (582 lb) |
| Engine and documented higher-weight change | 1,150 kg (2,535 lb) | 685 kg (1,510 lb) | 465 kg (1,025 lb) | 364.2 kg (803 lb) |

The assumed fuel weighs **140 × 0.72 = 100.8 kg (222 lb)**. In the engine-only case, empty weight rises by **25 kg (55 lb)**, while the limit stays the same. The people-and-bags allowance therefore falls by 25 kg.

In the higher-weight case, the limit rises by **110 kg (243 lb)**, but empty weight rises by **35 kg (77 lb)** from the original case. The net allowance increases by **110 − 35 = 75 kg (165 lb)**. The advertised weight-limit increase and the payload increase are different numbers.

These cases isolate weight arithmetic. They don't establish centre of gravity, baggage limits, fuel required for a trip or runway performance. Nor does the example's fuel quantity represent full tanks or a required reserve for a real airplane. Our [four-seat loading guide](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) shows how to continue from an initial screening calculation to a complete mission assessment.

## Check the propeller and engine details

Treat the propeller as part of the configuration, not an incidental accessory. Air Plains' [172 product page](https://www.airplains.com/cessna172-180hp) lists specific engine/propeller combinations and fixed-pitch options. Identify the actual propeller model, any applicable pitch or diameter information, and the documents supporting it before using a claimed cruise or climb figure.

A comparison is easier to follow when each number has a setting beside it. Request the configuration, power setting, altitude, mixture basis and measurement method behind a seller's reported fuel flow or speed. Keep an unsupported number marked unconfirmed; don't convert a casual report into a planning input.

For an aircraft already converted, ask which components were installed new, overhauled, inspected or reused. Put their dates and hours beside the conversion date. The work described by “engine conversion” doesn't tell you how old each accessory is or what its records establish.

Engine condition deserves its own review. A recently modified airplane can still raise calendar-age, storage or maintenance questions. The [low-hours, old-overhaul guide](/blog/low-engine-hours-old-overhaul) explains the evidence to request without treating engine hours as a condition verdict.

## Read performance evidence by its status

Air Plains' [separate performance-data page](https://www.airplains.com/post/172-180hp-performance-data) explicitly describes its downloadable data as reference only, for aircraft at **1,157 kg (2,550 lb)**. That is a different purpose from establishing which approved material applies to a particular aircraft.

Approval status can also vary within a document: page 13 of the [172051 reference copy](https://yokota374fss.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/airplane_flight_manual-172051.pdf) labels its cruise-fuel table “Not FAA Approved.” Check the status of the relevant page as well as the title on the cover.

For each departure comparison, write down the source, applicable configuration, weight, atmospheric conditions, runway assumptions and technique. Separate ground roll from obstacle-clearance distance. If a required case is outside the documented data, record it as unresolved and obtain guidance rather than filling the gap with another model's chart.

A statement such as “better than stock” doesn't quantify the margin for your departure. Ask what evidence resolves the actual question: this runway, this loading and these conditions. An engine rating or brochure percentage can't do that calculation on its own.

## Build a conversion records file

[§91.417](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.417) describes the maintenance and alteration records owners/operators must keep, including work descriptions, completion dates and return-to-service approval information. Your buying assessment needs the records that connect the approval to the installed airplane.

Request these before agreeing on the [prebuy inspection scope](/blog/prebuy-vs-annual-inspection):

* The applicable STC identification, authorization and installation documents, plus the aircraft's alteration and return-to-service records.
* Current basic manual and installed supplements, with model/serial applicability and revision information.
* Weight-and-balance record, equipment list and an account of later changes.
* Engine, propeller and accessory identification, hours, dates and maintenance records.
* Evidence resolving required structural, equipment, marking and placard changes for the installed approvals.

These are proposed evidence requests, not a declaration that a seller has supplied them. Ask the inspecting mechanic which record or physical check will answer each unresolved item. An emailed STC reference sheet alone doesn't demonstrate the complete installation.

## Check pilot and insurer requirements

The high-performance-airplane provision in [§61.31(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.31) uses an engine rating of **more than 149 kW (200 hp)**. The rule's exact threshold is more than 200 hp; a 180 hp rating doesn't trigger that provision by itself. Other qualifications and aircraft-specific requirements still need checking.

Identify the actual airplane and the qualifications for the intended operation rather than treating a conversion's marketing label as an endorsement category. A more powerful installation still needs an aircraft-specific review of the documents and your qualifications.

Ask your instructor for a checkout covering the applicable documents and installed controls. Give the insurer the actual configuration and ask for its written pilot, checkout and coverage requirements. No policy terms or premium quotation were obtained for this article.

If you're choosing an airplane for sport-pilot privileges, check the separate aircraft and pilot criteria in the [MOSAIC guide](/blog/sport-pilot-private-pilot-mosaic-aircraft). A conversion name doesn't establish eligibility for that operation either.

## Compare a converted 172 with a 182

Put actual candidates side by side using the same people, bags, departure airport and trip. Recalculate fuel for each candidate's documented configuration; don't use one fuel quantity for both merely to make the table simpler.

Record the questions that could change the choice:

| Mission question | Evidence to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Can it carry the recurring group? | Actual weights, current empty-weight basis, limits and loading envelope |
| Can it make the departure? | Applicable takeoff and climb information for the planned conditions |
| Will it need a fuel stop? | Credible trip-fuel and reserve calculation, with documented fuel capacity |
| What changes the workload? | Installed controls, avionics, operating documents and checkout requirements |
| What affects the ownership budget? | Aircraft-specific maintenance findings and dated written estimates |

If a documented converted 172 covers your recurring mission with the margins you choose, it can stay on the shortlist. If it leaves your passenger, fuel or departure questions unresolved, a different candidate deserves examination. A more expensive airplane also needs the same evidence; its model name doesn't complete the assessment.
