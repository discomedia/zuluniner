# Cherokee Six vs a four-seat single: how much payload do you gain?

A Cherokee Six can leave considerably more capacity for people and bags than a four-seat trainer. The size of that gain depends on the individual airplane's empty weight and the fuel its mission requires. Two Sixes can give different answers even before you compare either with a 172.

Start with the same passenger group and destination plan, then put each candidate's current weight records beside its fuel requirement. A six-seat cabin answers the seating question. Your loading calculation answers whether that group, their baggage and the planned fuel fit together.

A [pilot asking why their club's Cherokee Six carried less than expected](https://www.reddit.com/r/flying/comments/kuwbk5/why_is_the_useful_load_for_a_19781979_piper/) captures the problem well. The discussion mixes full tanks, equipment weight and assumptions about other airplanes. Below, three fictional candidates make those assumptions visible. Research and available classifieds were checked on **19 October 2026**.

## Compare the exact variants

This comparison concerns a **Cessna 172S-type four-seat airplane** and the **fixed-gear PA-32-300 Cherokee Six**. It doesn't transfer loading limits to a PA-32R Lance or Saratoga, a later 6X, or every PA-32-260. The model, serial number, approved modifications and installed equipment belong at the top of the comparison sheet.

For a current factory reference, [Textron's Skyhawk specifications](https://cessna.txtav.com/en/piston/cessna-skyhawk) list four occupants, a maximum takeoff weight of **1,157 kg (2,550 lb)** and **201 L (53 US gal)** of usable fuel. These published figures identify a configuration; they don't establish the empty weight of a used 172 you might buy.

For the Six, the [Piper PA-32-300 Information Manual, part 761-559](https://www.piperowner.org/talk/uploads/editor/j8/881ruic1xaxw.pdf), issued in July 1973, provides a historical reference. Its weight-and-balance material uses a maximum of **1,542 kg (3,400 lb)**. Its applicability page limits the document to specified serial numbers and warns that operational use requires current revisions.

This scanned copy is reference material, not confirmation of today's revision status or the approved documents for an advertised aircraft. [Piper explains how to obtain serial-specific manuals and check revisions](https://www.piper.com/technical-publications/). The worked cases below use chosen assumptions, not a claim that any listing matches that manual's applicability.

## Put the weight figures on the same basis

Ask what the quoted empty weight already includes. Equipment, unusable fuel and oil definitions can make apparently comparable numbers differ. A historic form and a modern spreadsheet can use different labels for the same underlying items.

The Piper reference gives a concrete example. On **report VB-551, page 5-7, revised 11 January 1974**, its form adds **10.2 kg (22.5 lb)** for **11.4 L (12 US qt)** of oil after the line labeled licensed empty weight. Its displayed carrying-capacity calculation subtracts that licensed empty figure from gross weight. Ignoring the separate oil line would overstate the capacity left for your trip load.

Don't add oil a second time if the current airplane's documented basic weight already includes it. Have the records establish the convention, then account for every item once. The [FAA's weight-and-balance chapter](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak/12_phak_ch10.pdf) explains why equipment changes need corresponding weight-record changes.

For all three fictional candidates here, the stated empty weight **already includes installed equipment, unusable fuel and the assumed full operating oil load**. Everything subsequently added is people, baggage or usable fuel. That explicit convention keeps the comparison independent of the old form's terminology.

## Give each airplane the fuel its mission needs

Comparing both airplanes with full tanks can answer an ownership question: what can I carry after a routine top-off? Comparing the same route needs a different calculation. It requires each airplane's departure fuel, including the fuel needed for the flight and the applicable planning allowances.

Use the individual aircraft's approved data, expected conditions and your operating plan to establish that fuel. A larger tank doesn't require you to fill it for every trip, and reducing fuel simply to fit the group doesn't establish that the resulting flight is adequately planned.

For the arithmetic below, assume a fictional trip needs **151 L (40 US gal)** of usable departure fuel in the 172-type candidate and **227 L (60 US gal)** in either Six. These quantities are chosen teaching inputs. They aren't fuel-burn predictions, a reserve policy, a range comparison or a recommendation for any route.

At an assumed avgas weight of **0.719 kg/L (6 lb/US gal)**, those quantities weigh about **109 kg (240 lb)** and **163 kg (360 lb)** respectively. The conversion is rounded; the calculations use pounds throughout and convert results afterward. Verify the fuel-weight basis required for the actual aircraft.

## Compare three fictional candidates

These are **invented teaching airplanes**, not inspected aircraft, factory-standard empty weights or figures extracted from the linked ads. Candidate A uses the 172S takeoff-weight limit identified above. B and C use the historical PA-32-300 reference limit. Their different empty weights are assumptions designed to isolate the effect of equipment and configuration.

| Teaching input | A: 172S-type | B: PA-32-300 | C: heavier PA-32-300 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Assumed takeoff limit | 1,157 kg (2,550 lb) | 1,542 kg (3,400 lb) | 1,542 kg (3,400 lb) |
| Assumed empty weight on the stated basis | 748 kg (1,650 lb) | 907 kg (2,000 lb) | 975 kg (2,150 lb) |
| Difference before usable fuel | 408 kg (900 lb) | 635 kg (1,400 lb) | 567 kg (1,250 lb) |
| Assumed departure fuel | 151 L (40 US gal) | 227 L (60 US gal) | 227 L (60 US gal) |
| Fuel weight | 109 kg (240 lb) | 163 kg (360 lb) | 163 kg (360 lb) |
| Capacity left for people and bags | **299 kg (660 lb)** | **472 kg (1,040 lb)** | **404 kg (890 lb)** |

B gains **172 kg (380 lb)** of people-and-baggage capacity over A for these chosen fuel loads. C gains **104 kg (230 lb)**. The extra **68 kg (150 lb)** of empty weight in C takes exactly that much away from B's result. The Six badge alone doesn't tell you which comparison applies.

Now specify the travelers. Four adults at **82 kg (180 lb)** each, plus **27 kg (60 lb)** of bags, total approximately **354 kg (780 lb)**. The rounded kilograms are for reading; four times 180 plus 60 is exactly 780 lb in the calculation.

| Result for that four-person group | A | B | C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Capacity after the assumed fuel | 299 kg (660 lb) | 472 kg (1,040 lb) | 404 kg (890 lb) |
| People and baggage | 354 kg (780 lb) | 354 kg (780 lb) | 354 kg (780 lb) |
| Weight margin | **54 kg (120 lb) over** | **118 kg (260 lb) below** | **50 kg (110 lb) below** |

A fails this weight screen. B and C pass only this screen; no balance, compartment, seating, runway or climb result has yet been established. Their numerical margins aren't allowances to exceed another limitation.

## Add the fifth and sixth occupants

The extra seats can change how you use the airplane, but they also change the load. Five adults at the same assumed weight, with the same bags, total **435 kg (960 lb)**. Six total **517 kg (1,140 lb)**.

At the assumed departure fuel, B has **36 kg (80 lb)** left with five adults, while C is **32 kg (70 lb)** over. With six adults, B is **45 kg (100 lb)** over and C is **113 kg (250 lb)** over. Neither Six carries that six-adult group at the selected fuel load, although both carried the original four-person group by weight.

This is why a Six can be a substantial four-person traveling upgrade without becoming a full-fuel, six-adult airplane. A family with lighter occupants could produce another result; so could heavier baggage. Enter actual traveler weights rather than substituting the illustrative adult weight above.

For another perspective, putting the current Skyhawk reference's full **201 L (53 US gal)** into fictional A adds **144 kg (318 lb)** of usable fuel. Its capacity for people and bags becomes **264 kg (582 lb)**, rather than 299 kg (660 lb). That is A's invented empty-weight calculation, not Textron's published full-fuel payload for its factory configuration.

## Check where the load goes

A total-weight pass leaves the center-of-gravity calculation unfinished. Seat assignments and baggage locations create different moments, even with the same total load. The two Six candidates above deliberately have no invented station arms or assumed center of gravity, so neither receives a balance approval.

For each real candidate, compute the loaded condition with the current aircraft records and the correct station data. Check the relevant stages of the flight as fuel is consumed. Confirm compartment and seat limits, the approved seating arrangement, restraints and how the intended bags can be secured. A cabin photograph doesn't establish those limits.

Then check performance for the weight and conditions you actually intend to use. The [FAA aircraft-performance chapter](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak/13_phak_ch11.pdf) explains the effects of weight and atmospheric conditions. A heavier airplane with a larger engine still needs its own takeoff and climb calculation. Our [density-altitude article](/blog/density-altitude-takeoff-climb-performance) develops that separate decision.

## Read a real listing without borrowing its certainty

An available [1977 Cherokee 6/300 listing](/aircraft/1977-piper-cherokee-6-300-2461466-trade-a-plane) advertises factory air conditioning, club seating, upgraded avionics and a carrying figure of **587 kg (1,294 lb)**. Those are seller-supplied claims, not a verified loading result. Request the current weight-and-balance report and equipment list, including the oil and fuel conventions behind that number.

That aircraft also appears in a syndicated ad with the same registration and serial number. Two ads don't create two independent measurements. Match identity before treating different marketplace pages as separate comparison candidates.

The records may support the advertised figure, or reveal a different basis. Equipment mentioned in an ad tells you what to investigate; it doesn't quantify the weight effect of each installation. No particular modification's penalty, aircraft condition or seller's loading practice is inferred here.

If your regular trip is four adults and bags, a documented Six may solve a capacity problem without using every seat. If six adults are a routine requirement, make that the first worked case and allow the fuel, balance and performance results to decide whether the candidate fits.
