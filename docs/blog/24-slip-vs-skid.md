# Slip vs skid in the base-to-final turn: what the rudder is doing

A slip and a skid describe uncoordinated flight. You can't identify either one just by seeing aileron and rudder applied in opposite directions. In a turn, you need to know how the airplane's bank and turning motion relate, and what the slip/skid indication shows.

That becomes a practical problem when a pilot overshoots the turn onto final. Trying to point the nose toward the runway with excess inside rudder can create a skid. If the wing also exceeds its critical angle of attack, the result can be a stall and spin close to the ground. An early go-around avoids trying to rescue that approach with increasingly distorted control inputs.

A [student's question about the base-to-final stall and spin](https://www.reddit.com/r/flying/comments/10d8jms/) drew replies that disagreed over whether opposite control inputs necessarily meant a slip. This US-first explanation uses the FAA's current [Airplane Flying Handbook catalog](https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook), checked on **24 October 2026**. The drawings and ground-study examples are original; they aren't flight-test results or instructions to demonstrate a stall.

## Separate bank, yaw and flightpath

Bank is the airplane's roll attitude: one wing is lower than the other. Yaw is rotation about the vertical axis, moving the nose left or right. Flightpath describes where the airplane is moving. A change in nose direction doesn't by itself establish that the airplane is following the path you want.

In [Chapter 3 of the FAA handbook](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/04_afh_ch3.pdf), the horizontal component of lift provides the turning force when the airplane is banked. Rudder helps coordinate the turn, including opposing adverse yaw associated with aileron use. It isn't a substitute for planning the turn's flightpath.

Ailerons also control roll rather than assigning a permanent bank angle to a control position. An airplane can already be banked left while the pilot applies right aileron. Looking at those two facts alone won't tell you whether it is slipping, coordinated or skidding.

For a ground explanation, separate three questions: Which way is the airplane banked? What is its motion through the air? What are the controls doing now? Keeping those questions separate prevents a control-position mnemonic from replacing an explanation of the aircraft's state.

## Compare coordination in a left turn

For this comparison, assume a left turn and a left bank. **Inside** means toward the turn, on the left; **outside** means to the right. The FAA describes a slip as excessive bank for the existing rate of turn. A skid represents the opposite mismatch: too much turning rate for the bank.

The slip/skid ball, or the corresponding electronic indication, helps identify coordination. In the ordinary left-turn comparison, a centered ball indicates coordination, a ball displaced left indicates a slip, and a ball displaced right indicates a skid. The handbook's [coordination discussion and Figure 3-13](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/04_afh_ch3.pdf) connect those indications with the sideways forces occupants feel.

## Follow the overshoot error chain

The FAA's [cross-control stall discussion in Chapter 5](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/06_afh_ch5.pdf) describes a base-to-final overshoot followed by excess rudder into the turn, opposing aileron and increasing back-elevator pressure. That combination can produce a skidding stall and rapid spin entry. The handbook identifies a go-around as the safest response to the overshoot.

Here is an **invented ground-study example**. A pilot in a left traffic pattern realizes the runway centerline is passing farther inside the turn than expected. They focus on getting the nose pointed toward the runway. As the nose yaws left, they use right aileron to resist an unwanted increase in bank, then pull to keep the nose from dropping.

Calling that a slip because the controls are opposed misses the developing skid. The right aileron doesn't erase the existing left bank, and the rudder input doesn't prove the airplane is following a tighter acceptable path. The pilot's attention has shifted from managing the approach to forcing a visual alignment.

For a briefing exercise, stop the story at the first recognized overshoot. Ask when the pilot could have discontinued the approach, what cues they overlooked and which aircraft-specific go-around procedure they should already know. The exercise ends with a decision to abandon the attempted landing, rather than a proposed control sequence for salvaging it.

## Keep angle of attack in the explanation

[Angle of attack](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-01/Angle%20of%20Attack%20Awareness.pdf), abbreviated **AOA**, is the angle between a wing's chord line and its relative airflow. The chord line runs from the airfoil's leading edge to its trailing edge. It isn't the same angle as the nose-to-path difference in the plan-view drawings above.

[Chapter 5's stall and spin discussion](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/06_afh_ch5.pdf) explains that a stall follows exceeding critical AOA, and that a stall in either a slipping or skidding turn can lead to a spin. A slip therefore doesn't provide immunity from a stall or spin. Coordination also doesn't remove the need to manage AOA.

Avoid reducing the whole explanation to one wing moving slower. Such a slogan leaves out the airplane's loading, yaw and AOA. It also encourages a misleading prediction that one control position guarantees a particular wing drop. The purpose of this comparison is recognizing the developing problem, not forecasting a recoverable outcome close to the ground.

Ask what the published stall speed actually represents before treating it as a boundary for every maneuver. Loading and flight condition matter. Our [maneuvering-speed and weight explanation](/blog/maneuvering-speed-aircraft-weight) connects aircraft weight and maneuvering assumptions, but neither that article nor a number on the airspeed indicator supplies a universal margin for an uncoordinated approach.

## Understand what an intentional slip is for

The FAA's [Chapter 9 discussion of intentional slips](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/10_afh_ch9.pdf) describes forward slips for increasing descent rate and sideslips used in crosswind landing technique. These are purposeful uses of uncoordinated flight within the airplane's limitations and the pilot's training. Their existence doesn't justify a skid to force a base-to-final turn.

Chapter 9 also notes that slipping flight can introduce airspeed-indication error in some airplanes and that aircraft-specific restrictions may concern fuel, duration or flap configuration. Check the applicable Airplane Flight Manual or Pilot's Operating Handbook, abbreviated **AFM/POH**, before applying a technique learned in another airplane. Don't assume the same flap setting or instrument picture transfers unchanged.

An intentional slip should have a defined purpose and an instructor-taught way to establish, monitor and discontinue it. A pilot who cannot explain what their slip is accomplishing has a question to resolve in training. Adding an improvised slip to an approach that is already deteriorating doesn't resolve that knowledge gap.

## Test the explanation on the ground

Use these **original study cases** without trying to reproduce them in flight. The stated indications are simplified inputs for discussion; the answers aren't control commands or a complete assessment of an actual airplane.

| In an ordinary left turn, the ball is displaced right. The pilot says it must be a slip because they are holding right aileron. | The supplied ball indication identifies a skid in this left-turn comparison. The pilot's label is inconsistent with that indication. | Why the mismatch developed and how an instructor would assess the full situation. |
| A pilot says a forward slip can't stall because they have seen an airplane tend to level its wings in a demonstration. | That observation doesn't establish immunity from a stall or spin. It cannot support a guarantee for another airplane or condition. | Applicable aircraft limitations, the instructor's explanation and the actual conditions of the demonstration. |

For each case, state the available evidence before giving the label. Then change the turn direction and explain what would change. If the answer relies only on remembering a pedal or stick position, return to the motion and coordination questions.

## Bring specific questions to an instructor

Take the diagrams to a ground briefing and ask the instructor to check your explanation. Discuss how the installed coordination display works, what aircraft-specific slip restrictions apply and how you will recognize an approach that should be discontinued. Our [flight-review preparation guide](/blog/flight-review-preparation) includes a way to record questions and follow-up work.

The FAA handbook's [approach and landing chapter](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/10_afh_ch9.pdf) discusses stabilized approaches and go-arounds. Use it with the aircraft's own procedures and your instructor's briefing. This article gives no universal pattern bank limit, practice altitude, stall-demonstration sequence or spin-recovery checklist.

No instructor interview, independent professional review or aircraft flight test was obtained for this article. Any in-air demonstration needs an appropriately qualified instructor, a suitable aircraft and a properly planned training environment. Recreating a base-to-final stall near the ground is outside this ground-study exercise.

## Check the airplane before transferring a technique

If you're moving to another type, ask how its controls, cockpit references and approved procedures change your training needs. A seller's description of pleasant handling can't answer those questions for you. Record them alongside the aircraft documents you request.

[Browse available aircraft](/aircraft) with a transition-training plan as part of the purchase. Check the candidate's manuals and installed equipment, then budget time with an instructor who knows that configuration. Understanding the slip/skid explanation should make that briefing more precise; it doesn't replace it.
