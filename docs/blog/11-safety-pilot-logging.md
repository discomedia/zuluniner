# Safety-pilot time: who can log PIC and cross-country?

Two pilots can sometimes log pilot-in-command time during the same flight. That doesn't give both pilots the same cross-country credit. Start with who is responsible for the flight, then identify the rule that permits each pilot's logbook entry and the requirement that entry is meant to satisfy.

For a common US practice flight, the pilot flying under a view-limiting device can log PIC as the appropriately rated sole manipulator of the controls. The safety pilot's entry depends on whether they also act as PIC, and covers only the period for which their safety-pilot presence is required. Cross-country credit needs a separate check.

The [question about safety-pilot cross-country time](https://www.pilotsofamerica.com/community/threads/can-a-safety-pilot-log-cross-country-time.116199/) regularly produces conflicting forum answers. The explanations below use current regulations and the FAA's own interpretations, checked on **18 October 2026**. The eCFR pages inspected display Title 14 as updated through 15 October 2026.

## Assign responsibility before choosing a logbook column

**Acting as PIC** means accepting command responsibility. The definition in [§1.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-1.1) identifies the person with final authority and responsibility for the operation and safety of the flight, designated as PIC and appropriately rated. That responsibility can't be created afterward by selecting a logbook category.

**Logging PIC** is governed separately by [§61.51(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.51). For the private pilots considered here, two relevant bases are being the appropriately rated sole manipulator of the controls, or acting as PIC while the regulations require more than one pilot. The latter condition can arise during simulated instrument flight.

Suppose Alex manipulates the controls while Blair is the safety pilot. If Blair is also the agreed, qualified acting PIC, Alex's sole-manipulator basis and Blair's required-crew PIC basis can overlap. Blair's command responsibility continues when Alex removes the hood, but Blair's logging basis may stop then.

The FAA's [Gebhart interpretation, dated 22 June 2009](https://www.faa.gov/media/14521), illustrates that limit. Its pilot flying logged the full flight as PIC; the acting-PIC safety pilot logged only the simulated-instrument portion. The letter's safety-pilot rule reference uses the numbering then in effect. Today's relevant simulated-flight provision is §91.109(c).

If Alex instead acts as PIC and Blair only performs the safety-pilot role, Blair doesn't gain a PIC entry from that role alone. [§61.51(f)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.51) provides a second-in-command, or **SIC**, logging basis for the appropriately rated required pilot during that portion. Check the actual basis rather than assuming every right-seat entry belongs in PIC.

## Identify when the safety pilot is required

For the ordinary private-pilot practice considered here, [§91.109(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-91.109) requires an appropriately rated safety pilot in the other control seat during simulated instrument flight. It also addresses the safety pilot's view and functioning controls. A friend somewhere in the cabin doesn't meet the other-control-seat requirement.

The role begins and ends with the relevant operation. Time spent taxiing, flying visually before the hood goes on, or approaching after it comes off doesn't automatically become the safety pilot's loggable time. Keep a record of the actual simulated-instrument segments.

Don't confuse logging SIC with imposing every rule for a designated airline-style SIC. In [Beaty, dated 23 January 2013](https://www.faa.gov/media/12606), the FAA distinguished a safety pilot in a single-pilot aircraft from a designated SIC requiring §61.55 qualifications, while expressly recognizing that safety-pilot time can be logged as SIC. The letter's facts and today's regulations still need to match the operation being evaluated.

Qualifications for acting PIC are another check. A category/class rating that permits a sole-manipulator entry doesn't settle every endorsement, currency or medical requirement for command. The scenarios below assume those qualifications are satisfied; they don't use logging permission to create operating privileges.

## Separate simulated instrument time from instruction

[§61.51(g)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.51) ties instrument time to operating solely by reference to instruments under actual or simulated instrument conditions. Looking outside for traffic as safety pilot doesn't earn simulated instrument time, even when the safety pilot can log PIC for another reason.

Receiving instruction is a separate category. Under §61.51(h), training time involves an authorized instructor and the required training record. Two private pilots practicing together don't turn their flight into dual instruction merely by calling one person the safety pilot.

For an instrument-airplane rating, [§61.65(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.65) specifies separate experience and training requirements. It includes 50 hours of cross-country PIC time, subject to its stated exception, and 40 hours of actual or simulated instrument time with specified instructor requirements. Time satisfying one column doesn't automatically satisfy the others. These practice scenarios don't replace the required instructor-led cross-country or other training.

## Name the cross-country requirement

[§61.1's cross-country definitions](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.1) vary with purpose. For the instrument-airplane experience examined here, the applicable definition includes navigation to a landing point **more than 92.6 km (50 NM)** straight-line distance from the original departure point. Exactly 50 NM doesn't exceed that threshold.

Flying a long route isn't enough. A circuitous flight can accumulate distance without reaching a qualifying landing point. Conversely, the distance test doesn't by itself establish that a particular person's role qualifies for cross-country PIC credit.

This article's scenario credit column refers specifically to the **50-hour cross-country PIC requirement in §61.65(d)(1)**. A trip to another airport exactly 50 NM away may fit a different cross-country definition while failing this one. Airline transport, sport-pilot and other experience requirements need their own checks; don't reuse this column as a universal cross-country rule.

## Work through six original scenarios

These are **fictional teaching cases**, not FAA exam questions or actual logbook entries. Alex and Blair both hold private airplane single-engine-land ratings, appropriate current FAA medical certificates, and all qualifications needed for their assigned roles. They fly a normally single-pilot airplane with functioning dual controls, by day under VFR in visual conditions. Neither provides instruction.

Alex manipulates the controls for the entire flight, including takeoff and landing. Blair never manipulates them. Each flight totals **1.4 hours**; whenever hood work is specified, the recorded simulated-instrument period totals **1.0 hour**, with the hood removed for takeoff and landing. Navigation to another landing point occurs where stated. All other applicable requirements are assumed satisfied.

| Original scenario | Acting PIC | Loggable time in this case | Credit toward §61.65(d)(1) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Local flight, 1.0 hour under the hood; return to departure airport without another landing | Alex | Alex: 1.4 PIC and 1.0 simulated instrument. Blair: 1.0 SIC. | Neither pilot has qualifying cross-country PIC credit. |
| 2. Same local flight, but Blair accepts command before departure | Blair | Alex: 1.4 PIC and 1.0 simulated instrument. Blair: 1.0 PIC. | Neither pilot has qualifying cross-country PIC credit. |
| 3. Blair accepts command, but Alex flies the whole local flight visually; no hood | Blair | Alex: 1.4 PIC. Blair: no loggable pilot time on these facts. | Neither pilot has qualifying cross-country PIC credit. |
| 4. Alex flies to and lands at an airport about 111 km (60 NM) away; 1.0 hour under the hood | Blair | Alex: 1.4 PIC and 1.0 simulated instrument. Blair: 1.0 PIC. | Alex: 1.4 cross-country PIC. Blair: none. |
| 5. Same arrangement, but the other landing point is exactly 92.6 km (50 NM) away | Blair | Alex: 1.4 PIC and 1.0 simulated instrument. Blair: 1.0 PIC. | Neither qualifies for this requirement: the distance isn't more than 50 NM. |
| 6. Fly about 111 km (60 NM) away, then return to the departure airport without landing elsewhere; 1.0 hour under the hood | Blair | Alex: 1.4 PIC and 1.0 simulated instrument. Blair: 1.0 PIC. | Neither qualifies for this requirement: there is no qualifying away landing. |

Cases 1–3 isolate the logging basis from command responsibility. In case 3, Blair's designation doesn't create a second required pilot during ordinary visual flight in this airplane. Alex still has the sole-manipulator PIC basis. Case 2 adds the required safety-pilot period, so Blair can log that period as PIC.

Cases 4–6 apply the distance and landing tests to a flight in which Alex does all the flying. The same 1.0-hour PIC entry for Blair appears in each, while Blair's cross-country result stays zero. Alex's instrument-rating cross-country result changes with the qualifying landing, not with the size of Blair's PIC entry.

## Check who conducted the entire flight

Gebhart directly addresses the tempting inference in case 4. Its safety pilot was required for only part of the flight, so the FAA denied that pilot cross-country credit. The pilot who conducted the entire qualifying flight received it. Logging PIC for the hood segment didn't transfer the whole flight's cross-country character to the safety pilot.

Don't repair that gap by informally dividing the takeoff, en route flying and landing between pilots and assuming both receive the same credit. Our cases deliberately keep Alex on the controls throughout. If you change those facts, have your instructor check the applicable interpretations and requirement before assigning credit.

The cases also assume VFR and visual weather. Filing IFR, encountering actual instrument conditions, adding an instructor or using a different aircraft can change which qualifications and logging provisions apply. Keep the facts with the answer rather than memorizing a sentence about two pilots logging PIC.

## Brief the roles and record the segments

Before departure, identify the acting PIC and confirm that person's qualifications for the intended flight. Agree who will manipulate the controls, when the view-limiting device may be used, how traffic hazards will be communicated, and the control-transfer procedure. Responsibility should be clear before a traffic conflict creates pressure to decide.

After landing, reconcile actual hood time and any changes in roles. Record the date, flight time, route, aircraft identity and relevant experience/conditions required by [§61.51(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.51). It calls for the safety pilot's name when required by §91.109. Record approach information where applicable rather than treating an instrument-time total as a complete recency record.

## Choose an airplane for the practice you intend

Before you [browse aircraft classifieds](/aircraft), identify the practice mission: suitable controls and sightlines, the installed navigation equipment you need, dispatch arrangements and the operating costs you can support. Ownership or a partnership doesn't alter the logging rules.

The [ownership-cost guide](/blog/airplane-ownership-cost-50-100-200-hours) helps structure the budget, and the [aircraft-loading guide](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) explains why two pilots, fuel and equipment still need a documented loading calculation. Pick an airplane and a practice arrangement that fit your flying, then credit only the experience the actual flight supports.
