# Fuel range in an aircraft ad vs fuel you can plan to use

An advertised range is the result of a particular loading, power setting, atmosphere and reserve policy. Before using it to shortlist an airplane, find those assumptions. Then build a fuel plan for your passengers, route and weather using the documentation for that aircraft.

You can't reliably turn a brochure radius into a trip limit by subtracting a fixed distance. A headwind changes distance covered per hour, a fuel stop changes the mission, and fuel needed after a diversion may be quite different from the reserve behind the advertised figure.

Airplane Academy's [written aircraft-buying guide](https://airplaneacademy.com/if-i-was-buying-a-plane-in-2026-heres-what-id-do/) treats load, seats, speed, range and equipment as parts of the same mission. That is the buying question here: what can this airplane carry over the trips you intend to make? This article uses manufacturer information and US rules checked on **29 October 2026**, followed by a clearly fictional fuel-budget exercise.

## Read the range footnote before the headline

Textron's current [Cessna Skyhawk product page](https://cessna.txtav.com/en/piston/cessna-skyhawk) lists **1,185 km (640 NM)** maximum range and **201 L (53 US gal)** usable fuel. Its linked [Skyhawk brochure, printed page 3](https://cessna.txtav.com/-/media/cessna/files/brochures/piston/skyhawk_brochure.pdf), explains the range basis: a ferry mission at long-range cruise, a standard day, no wind and a 45-minute VFR reserve.

That reserve is already in this published range. Treating every advertised number as a flight to empty tanks, or automatically subtracting another 45 minutes from it, would misread this example. Other advertisements may use different assumptions or omit them.

The same page lists maximum cruise speed separately. It doesn't establish that maximum range and maximum cruise speed occur together. Nor does a ferry-mission figure demonstrate the range with your passengers and baggage.

These are identified current-model manufacturer figures, not performance evidence for every older 172 or modified aircraft. For a particular airplane, obtain its applicable Pilot's Operating Handbook or Airplane Flight Manual, amendments, supplements and current loading records. Don't transfer this brochure's figures to an older aircraft simply because both ads say “Skyhawk.”

## Start with usable fuel actually aboard

**Total fuel capacity** and **usable fuel capacity** answer different questions. The usable quantity excludes fuel that the applicable aircraft documentation doesn't make available for the stated operating conditions. An ad should identify which figure it gives and which tanks are fitted.

Capacity also isn't the quantity aboard for departure. You may choose less fuel to meet loading limits, and fuel used during start and taxi won't still be aboard at takeoff. Establish a known usable starting quantity using the aircraft's prescribed checks; don't substitute the last receipt or an unverified cockpit entry for that check.

The FAA's [aircraft-performance guidance](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/fly-safe-addressing-ga-safety-1) connects passengers and cargo with the fuel that can be carried, then with weather and range. For your own loading calculation, use current aircraft records and the correct fuel-weight basis. Our [four seats, full fuel mission worksheet](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) explains that separate weight-and-balance problem.

Keep quantities consistent. A calculation in litres must use litres per hour. If the source uses gallons, establish whether they are US or imperial gallons before converting. Tank size alone doesn't tell you the usable fuel or the permitted loading.

## Separate the legal reserve from your planning margin

For US civil airplanes under the usual Part 91 rules, these fuel provisions are starting points:

| Operation | Fuel provision to account for |
| --- | --- |
| VFR by day | Under [§91.151](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-B/section-91.151), enough for the first intended landing point, considering wind and forecast weather, then at least 30 minutes at normal cruising speed. |
| VFR at night | The same section requires at least 45 minutes after that point, at normal cruising speed. |
| Flight in IFR conditions | [§91.167](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-B/section-91.167) accounts for the first intended airport, the alternate unless its stated exception applies, then 45 minutes at normal cruising speed. Weather reports, forecasts and conditions enter the calculation. |

VFR means visual flight rules; IFR means instrument flight rules. This is an airplane-focused summary, not the helicopter provisions or a complete dispatch determination. Read the conditions and exceptions in the linked sections for the proposed operation.

A reserve expressed in minutes must be translated into fuel at the applicable consumption. It isn't a fixed number of kilometres or nautical miles. Extra fuel for an expected delay or a planned diversion also shouldn't be silently counted as the same fuel that you intend to protect as reserve.

[§91.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-B/section-91.103) requires preflight familiarity with available flight information. For IFR flights or flights beyond an airport's vicinity, its specified information includes weather, fuel requirements, available alternatives and known ATC delays. Meeting one reserve number doesn't complete that work. Set any additional personal margin explicitly alongside the legal calculation.

## Build a fuel budget before drawing a radius

The following is an **invented ground-study example**, not Skyhawk performance data, a real flight plan or an approved dispatch calculation. Assume a fictional piston single with **160 L (42.3 US gal) of known usable fuel aboard before start**. The quantities below are teaching assumptions, not measured consumption or recommendations for another airplane.

For this simplified VFR exercise, protect **30 L (7.9 US gal)** as a chosen reserve. At the assumed normal-cruise consumption of **40 L/h (10.6 US gal/h)**, that represents 45 minutes. Keep another **10 L (2.6 US gal)** aside as a separate additional allowance. Neither amount is established as adequate for a real route.

| Fuel-budget item | Hypothetical quantity |
| --- | --- |
| Start and taxi | 4 L (1.1 US gal) |
| Takeoff and climb | 12 L (3.2 US gal) |
| Descent, approach and landing | 4 L (1.1 US gal) |
| Separate additional allowance | 10 L (2.6 US gal) |
| Protected reserve | 30 L (7.9 US gal) |
| Fuel left for the cruise segment | 100 L (26.4 US gal) |

The litre values sum to 160. Independently rounded US-gallon values won't necessarily sum exactly.

The cruise calculation is **100 L ÷ 40 L/h = 2.5 hours**. If the assumed still-air groundspeed is **200 km/h (108 kt)**, that allocates **500 km (270 NM)** to the cruise segment. It does **not** establish the total trip distance: climb and descent have their own time, fuel and distance, which must be included in a complete route calculation.

Why not divide 160 by 40 and claim four hours of travel? That spends fuel allocated to other phases, the separate allowance and the reserve. Why not multiply a brochure's maximum speed by those four hours? The power setting and consumption might not match, even before wind is considered.

A real calculation needs applicable aircraft performance data for each phase and the planned altitude, temperature, weight, mixture and power setting. Include the actual route, expected arrival or departure procedures, and fuel for applicable alternatives. This schematic exercise does not demonstrate compliance with the IFR fuel rule; it has no destination-to-alternate segment.

## Change the wind and loading assumptions

Hold the fictional airplane's airspeed and cruise consumption constant to isolate two effects. These assumptions are for arithmetic only; actual performance can change with loading and conditions.

| Original study case | Cruise-segment result |
| --- | --- |
| 160 L aboard; no wind; 200 km/h groundspeed | 100 L available for cruise; 2.5 hours; 500 km (270 NM). |
| Same fuel, with a direct headwind of 40 km/h (22 kt) | Groundspeed becomes 160 km/h (86 kt); the same 2.5 cruise hours cover 400 km (216 NM). |
| Only 130 L (34.3 US gal) aboard, with that same headwind | Keep the other allocations unchanged: 70 L (18.5 US gal) remain for cruise, giving 1.75 hours and 280 km (151 NM). |

The headwind case has the same cruise endurance but less distance covered. The reduced-fuel case has less of both. For the third case, assume that an independent loading check permits that quantity; removing fuel by itself doesn't prove weight-and-balance compliance.

This is why “it has a 640-NM range” doesn't answer a passenger-carrying mission question. The table isn't a recalculation of the manufacturer's Skyhawk claim. It demonstrates the arithmetic that an aircraft-specific fuel plan must resolve.

For a headwind directly along the intended track, the simple subtraction above is sufficient. A crosswind also requires a wind correction and changes the along-track component; subtracting the full wind speed would give the wrong result. Our [IAS, TAS and groundspeed article](/blog/ias-tas-groundspeed) works through that geometry separately.

Now add a study question: an unplanned 15 minutes at the example's cruise consumption costs **10 L (2.6 US gal)**. That equals the entire separate allowance in this ledger. You cannot still count those same litres as available for a second delay. For a real trip, revise expected arrival fuel and available options using actual consumption and conditions, rather than treating the original arithmetic as a promise.

## Keep checking the fuel plan in flight

The FAA's [performance and fuel-planning guidance](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/fly-safe-addressing-ga-safety-1) advises monitoring fuel during the flight and checking en route fuel availability before departure. The plan needs observations, not just an attractive radius on a screen.

At planned checks, compare elapsed time, position, fuel indications and any applicable fuel-flow or remaining-fuel calculation with the expected values. Establish what an installed display actually measures or calculates and follow its documentation. A remaining-fuel prediction is only as reliable as the inputs and assumptions behind it.

A lower groundspeed can increase time needed for the remaining route without changing the engine's hourly consumption. A routing change can add distance. A fuel stop that closes or lacks the required fuel changes the available alternatives. Reassess these developments early, while practical options remain.

Choose decision points as part of preflight planning: where will you check the prediction, and what change would require a shorter leg or a different stop? This article doesn't prescribe a universal fuel quantity or continuation rule. The aircraft, current conditions and available landing options must supply those decisions.

## Ask the seller for the missing inputs

A range claim becomes comparable when you can trace its inputs. Ask for the source of the number and the aircraft configuration to which it applies, rather than a seller's assurance that it “goes a long way.”

* Which manual, supplement or manufacturer document supports the figure? What revision and configuration does it describe?
* Is the fuel figure total capacity, usable capacity or fuel actually carried for the quoted mission? Which tank arrangement and modifications apply?
* What load, power setting, altitude, atmosphere, wind and reserve policy produced the range? Does it include the relevant flight phases?
* Are claimed cruise speed and consumption from the same operating point? If the seller reports experience, what conditions and records support it?
* With your passengers and bags, what fuel can legally be carried within the actual aircraft's loading envelope?

Download the original [aircraft-range comparison worksheet](/blog/aircraft-range-fuel-worksheet.md) to keep those answers beside a phase-by-phase fuel budget. Blank entries mean missing evidence, not zero fuel burn. It is a screening worksheet, not an approved flight-planning application.

## Compare airplanes on the same mission

Use the same people, bags, route, weather assumptions and reserve policy when comparing two aircraft. Then use each airplane's own applicable performance and loading data. A longer tank endurance doesn't automatically give a faster trip, and an extra fuel stop may change the comparison more than a small cruise-speed advantage.

Our [Cherokee Six payload comparison](/blog/cherokee-six-vs-four-seat-payload) shows how configuration affects the carrying side of this question. When you [browse available aircraft](/aircraft), keep a shortlist of aircraft whose documented configuration can support the mission, and retain the source behind every range claim.

Before buying, the question to resolve is specific: with this aircraft's actual loading and performance data, what fuel stops and margins would your recurring trip require? A brochure footnote starts that assessment. A documented mission calculation and aircraft-specific familiarization finish it.
