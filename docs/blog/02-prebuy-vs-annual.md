# Fresh annual vs prebuy: what are you actually buying?

A fresh annual is evidence of a required inspection and its recorded result. Before buying the airplane, you still need an independent assessment of its records, condition and likely ownership commitments. You can commission that assessment alongside an annual, but you have to agree on the buyer's questions and the work needed to answer them.

The phrase “prebuy inspection” doesn't tell you how much the mechanic will inspect. A logbook review, a brief examination with the cowling removed and a detailed inspection of known trouble areas can all carry that name. Your protection starts with a written scope, an inspector who knows the type and a purchase agreement that gives you time to act on the findings.

This guide covers conventional US type-certificated piston airplanes operated privately under Part 91. Experimental aircraft and aircraft on other inspection programs need different arrangements. Regulatory references were checked on 9 October 2026.

## What an annual establishes

For an airplane subject to the ordinary annual requirement, [14 CFR §91.409(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.409) requires an annual inspection within the preceding 12 calendar months and approval for return to service. The section includes exceptions and other inspection programs. The requirement follows the applicable aircraft and operation; transferring ownership doesn't restart its inspection clock.

The annual has a prescribed minimum scope. Under [§43.15(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/section-43.15), the inspector must use a checklist that includes the applicable scope and detail in [Appendix D to Part 43](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/appendix-Appendix%20D%20to%20Part%2043). Appendix D covers the airframe, engine, landing gear, controls and other systems, including inspections for deterioration and defects. An annual is substantial work when performed properly.

Ask for the actual inspection entry. [§43.11](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/section-43.11) specifies entries for approval or disapproval for return to service. An airplane can receive an annual inspection and have discrepancies that prevent approval. “Annual completed” in an advertisement therefore needs the entry, any discrepancy list and records of subsequent corrective work behind it.

A buyer asks further questions: How convincing is the repair history? What scheduled work falls due during my first year? Does the installed equipment suit my flying? What expensive areas were inaccessible, and what uncertainty am I accepting?

Those are purchase decisions. Neither the annual sign-off nor a prebuy report predicts how long a component will last. A clean inspection also can't reveal every defect without limits on access, disassembly and testing.

## Read the records before you travel

Start by asking the seller for legible scans of the available airframe, engine and propeller logs, including the most recent inspection entries. Request the aircraft's exact model, serial number, installed engine and propeller identities, current time readings and how each reading relates to the recorded time in service.

Ask for these supporting documents:

- The current airworthiness-directive compliance record, including recurring requirements and next due actions.
- Major repair and alteration records, relevant FAA Form 337s, supplemental type certificate documents and instructions for continued airworthiness.
- The current weight-and-balance report, equipment list and applicable flight-manual supplements.
- Overhaul invoices and work descriptions, recent maintenance invoices, component replacement dates and any open discrepancy list.
- Available engine-monitor data, oil-analysis history, previous inspection photographs and explanations of long storage periods.

This request deliberately goes beyond the minimum records a seller may be required to retain. [§91.417](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-E/section-91.417) requires continuing records of total time, life-limited-part status, inspection status, applicable airworthiness directives and certain other information. Its continuing-status records must transfer with the aircraft when sold; routine work records have different retention provisions. Don't describe every missing historical invoice as a regulatory breach.

Have your mechanic compare the documents with one another. An equipment installation should connect to its approval basis, maintenance instructions and any effect on weight and balance. An “overhauled engine” entry should connect to the engine identity and a description of the work, rather than just a time figure copied into an advertisement.

For airworthiness directives, usually called ADs, ask the mechanic to establish applicability to the installed airframe, engine, propeller and appliances. A spreadsheet from the seller is a starting point. The assessment needs the correct model and serial numbers, the compliance method and evidence supporting any “not applicable” conclusion.

An unexplained gap is a question to investigate. Ask what happened during that period, whether other records exist and whether the gap prevents establishing required status. If the answer changes your budget or confidence, resolve it before paying for travel and further inspection.

You should also check whether the airplane can carry your actual passengers, baggage and mission fuel. The [four-seats and full-fuel worksheet](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) helps you assess that before commissioning maintenance work on an aircraft that won't suit your trips.

## Choose the person who will examine the airplane

Choose and pay an inspector who reports directly to you. Ask them to disclose previous work on the aircraft and financial or business relationships with the seller or broker. The seller's regular shop may explain the history well, but an independent shop gives you another assessment of that history.

Ask about recent work on the exact model and configuration. Someone experienced with a fixed-gear Cherokee may still need different equipment and expertise for a retractable model. A converted Cessna may require documents and maintenance instructions beyond the original aircraft manual.

An A&P is a mechanic with airframe and powerplant ratings. IA means inspection authorization, an additional FAA authorization whose privileges include performing annual inspections under [§65.95](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-65/subpart-D/section-65.95). A stand-alone buyer's assessment doesn't acquire an annual's regulatory status because an IA conducts it. If you're commissioning an annual, confirm who has authority to perform and approve that work; [§43.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/section-43.7) also addresses return-to-service authority for appropriately rated repair stations.

Discuss what the shop can inspect itself and what requires a specialist. Avionics troubleshooting, composite repairs and nondestructive testing may need separate people. Confirm their availability before setting a short inspection deadline.

Ask for a sample report with identifying details removed. It should describe evidence, access limitations and next steps. A page of ticks gives you less to work with than photographs, relevant document references and specific unresolved questions.

## Write a scope around this aircraft

Build the scope in stages. First review records and inspect the areas most likely to make you reject the aircraft. Then authorize the remaining work if the initial findings support continuing. [Savvy Aviation's buyer-assistance process](https://www.savvyaviation.com/buyers-assistance/) describes tailored, two-phase checklists intended to identify major problems early. That's one specialist's workflow, rather than an FAA definition of a prebuy.

Specify access. “Check for corrosion” leaves too much unanswered: which panels come off, which interior areas are exposed and which structures remain hidden? Ask the mechanic to name the access points and any further disassembly that a finding might trigger.

Type knowledge changes those choices. Savvy's published [Cessna 172/182 and 170/180 checklist](https://www.savvyaviation.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Savvy-Cessna-172-182-170-180-series-prebuy-checklist.pdf) includes examining the firewall, engine mount and exhaust alongside engine diagnostics. It illustrates how a scope can identify specific areas; it isn't a replacement for current aircraft maintenance data or an inspection plan tailored to the candidate.

For a Piper, have the inspector consult [Piper's current technical publications](https://www.piper.com/technical-publications/) and applicable FAA directives for its exact model, serial number and history. Ask which structural inspections need specialist equipment and whether the supporting records establish when they are due. Avoid applying one Cherokee's inspection requirements to the entire PA-28 family.

For a Cirrus, include the records and scheduled work for the installed parachute system, relevant components and aircraft configuration. The [Cirrus technical-publications suite](https://store.cirrusaircraft.com/sr-series-online-technical-publications-suite/5637342579.p) is a route to the appropriate manuals. Have the shop identify the applicable revision and schedule; a generic model-year checklist can't establish an individual aircraft's due dates.

Engine assessment deserves its own agreed scope. Ask which manufacturer procedures govern compression testing and what borescope images, filter examination and historical data the mechanic will consider. [Lycoming explains why low operating hours can conceal deterioration associated with inactivity](https://www.lycoming.com/content/low-time-engine-may-not-mean-quality-and-value). The [engine-hours and calendar-age guide](/blog/low-engine-hours-old-overhaul) explores that purchasing question further.

Record limits beside the tests. A cylinder borescope doesn't establish the condition of every internal engine part. A brief ground run can't demonstrate every avionics function in flight. Ask what additional work could narrow each material uncertainty and what that work would cost.

## Agree on purchase conditions before opening panels

Make sure your purchase agreement permits the agreed inspection, access to records and any aircraft movement. It should give you a workable deadline for receiving findings and deciding whether to proceed. Weather, specialist availability and delayed documents can all consume that time.

Have an aviation transaction lawyer draft or review provisions for acceptance, rejection, deposits, repairs and extensions. These depend on the contract and applicable law. Agree explicitly who pays inspection invoices, transportation, reassembly and work needed if the sale stops. A defect report doesn't automatically require the seller to repair the airplane or refund a deposit.

Keep the technical inspection separate from title work. The [FAA's clear-title guidance](https://www.faa.gov/licenses_certificates/aircraft_certification/aircraft_registry/clear_titles) explains that its records contain qualifying liens and security documents filed with the Registry, while state law determines lien and security interests. A mechanic's report can't resolve ownership or every possible lien. Arrange the relevant title search and closing checks separately.

Also decide whether anyone will fly the aircraft, who that pilot will be and whether insurance covers the arrangement. Under [§91.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-A/section-91.7), an unairworthy aircraft can't simply fly home because it has a recent annual entry. A [special flight permit under §21.197](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-21/subpart-H/section-21.197) may be available for specified purposes when an aircraft is capable of safe flight; issuance and conditions require assessment.

## Buyer-to-mechanic scope worksheet

Copy this into your initial request. Ask the shop to complete the scope and quote before you authorize work. It's a briefing sheet, not an aircraft maintenance checklist or a purchase contract.

| Item | Buyer and inspector to complete |
| --- | --- |
| Aircraft identity | Registration, model, serial number, engine/propeller identities, major modifications, location. |
| Intended flying | Typical trips, expected yearly hours, instrument flight rules (IFR) or visual flight rules (VFR) use, equipment you need working. |
| Known history | Seller disclosures, storage periods, repairs, missing records, advertised inspection and overhaul dates. |
| Independence | Who commissions and pays; prior work on this aircraft; relationships requiring disclosure. |
| Stage 1 | Records to review; expensive type-specific areas to examine first; access and tests included. |
| Stage 2 | Remaining structural, engine, system and equipment checks; specialist work; flight evaluation if agreed. |
| Technical basis | Applicable maintenance publications and revisions; AD applicability assessment; modification instructions. |
| Limits | Areas inaccessible, components not opened, equipment not tested and reasons. |
| Authority to spend | Initial cap in USD; next approval point; person who can authorize extra work. |
| Stop conditions | Call before further work for unresolved structural damage, required-status gaps or other buyer-specified findings. |
| Deliverables | Written report, photographs, supporting references, urgency, repair estimates and open questions. |
| Completion | Reassembly, required maintenance entries, custody of records, delivery date and responsible contacts. |

Include a sentence such as: “Please contact me after Stage 1, before exceeding the agreed cap or before removing additional components. Separate observed findings from suspected causes, and show which items need further investigation.”

A spending cap controls authorization; it doesn't guarantee that the whole inspection will fit within that amount. Agree on how the shop will leave the aircraft if you stop. Maintenance performed during the assessment still needs the applicable records under [§43.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-43/section-43.9); calling the visit a prebuy doesn't remove those obligations.

## Read findings as purchase decisions

Ask the inspector to separate airworthiness or compliance findings, future maintenance commitments, mission equipment problems and cosmetic issues. Each needs evidence and a next action. Counting defects treats a worn seat and unresolved structural corrosion as equivalent.

The following is a **hypothetical findings example**, created for this article. It describes no aircraft inspected by ZuluNiner, and the cost figures are illustrative assumptions rather than quotations.

| Finding in the hypothetical report | What remains unresolved | Buyer decision checkpoint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corrosion visible at a structural attachment; photograph supplied. | Extent, permissible limits and repair method need further assessment. | Pause; obtain a specialist assessment before agreeing to a price adjustment. |
| A recurring AD appears on the status sheet without evidence supporting the stated next due time. | Actual compliance status. | Request the underlying entry and inspector's determination; don't price an unknown requirement as routine work. |
| Engine has low recorded hours but a long undocumented storage period. | Preservation history and internal condition beyond inspected areas. | Agree on further diagnostic work or decline the remaining uncertainty. |
| Required-for-mission autopilot function fails the agreed evaluation. | Repair route, parts and downtime. | Obtain a written repair estimate before deciding whether the aircraft still meets the mission. |
| Upholstery is worn; no related technical defect identified. | Cost of the buyer's preferred refurbishment. | Treat as a discretionary ownership expense. |

Suppose the hypothetical shop estimates USD 3,000 for one defined repair. If the buyer then allows USD 1,000 for an equipment fault and USD 2,000 for near-term scheduled work, the planned outlay becomes USD 6,000. Those assumptions don't cap an unresolved structural or engine problem, and a USD 6,000 discount wouldn't establish its condition.

Compare the evidence with your budget and tolerance for downtime. Buying may make sense when the remaining work has a defined repair route and fits your finances. Renegotiation may make sense when estimates change the economics. Walking away may make sense when access, records or diagnosis can't reduce uncertainty enough for you to accept it.

## When combining a prebuy and annual makes sense

Combining the work may reduce repeated disassembly if the annual is due soon and the shop can satisfy both scopes. Agree on that before inspection starts, including the authorized annual inspector, the buyer's additional questions, the work already covered and who pays if you reject the aircraft.

If an annual was signed last week, your inspector may tailor the buyer's assessment around its records and the remaining questions. They should explain what evidence supports reducing any work. The word “fresh” alone doesn't establish the scope you need.

When you [compare aircraft for sale](/aircraft), ask for records early and keep a candidate's unanswered questions beside its asking price. Commission the next inspection stage only when it can resolve a question that affects your purchase decision.
