# Four seats, full fuel: will this airplane carry your mission?

A four-seat airplane can carry four people only if their actual weights, bags and trip fuel fit its weight limits and centre-of-gravity envelope. Then it must have enough runway and climb performance for the departure. The seat count answers how many people can sit inside; the loading calculation answers whether your group can make this trip.

For a buyer, that's a question to settle before travelling to inspect an airplane. A beautiful panel won't compensate for leaving a passenger behind on the trips you bought it to fly.

This guide uses US operating rules, checked on 9 October 2026. The worked airplane below is entirely fictional: its weights, arms, limits and fuel estimates are teaching assumptions, not specifications for a Cessna, Piper or any other model. Use the particular aircraft's approved documents and current records for flight planning; [14 CFR §91.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-A/section-91.9) requires compliance with the applicable operating limitations.

## Start with the airplane's records

Two aircraft of the same model can have different empty weights and centres of gravity. Installed equipment and modifications change those numbers. The FAA's [Pilot's Handbook, Chapter 10](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak/12_phak_ch10.pdf) explains why current weight-and-balance records and the equipment list belong together.

Ask the seller for:

* The current empty-weight, arm or moment record, with its date and aircraft identity.
* The equipment list and records accounting for subsequent installations and removals.
* The applicable Pilot's Operating Handbook or Airplane Flight Manual, including its revision status and required supplements.
* The loading envelope, approved fuel quantities, baggage restrictions and performance data for the installed configuration.

A generic handbook downloaded for the same model doesn't establish these facts. Piper, for example, [describes its Pilot Information Manuals](https://www.piper.com/technical-publications/) as non-serialized reference versions and orders replacement flight manuals by airplane serial number.

An engine conversion may change weight, balance or performance assumptions. Check the approval and flight-manual supplement rather than applying the original airplane's figures to the modified one. The FAA's [Chapter 9 on aircraft documents](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/11_phak_ch9.pdf) explains the role of supplements for installed equipment.

If the seller supplies an old loading sheet with no explanation of later equipment changes, the carrying capacity is unconfirmed. Your [prebuy inspection scope](/blog/prebuy-vs-annual-inspection) should include resolving that gap.

## Separate carrying capacity from passenger payload

In this article, **available load** means the applicable maximum loaded weight minus the aircraft's documented basic empty weight. It is the allowance for the loads not already included in that empty-weight basis. Fuel uses part of it; people and bags use the rest.

**Payload** here means occupants, baggage and cargo. For a quick screening calculation:

**Payload allowance = maximum loaded weight − basic empty weight − fuel weight − other loads excluded from empty weight.**

Check the manual's definitions before using this equation. Empty-weight conventions differ, especially in older documentation: don't add oil or unusable fuel twice, or omit them where the record excludes them. The [FAA Weight and Balance Handbook](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2023-09/Weight_Balance_Handbook.pdf) explains the different weight terms.

Here's our fictional four-seat airplane:

| Teaching assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Maximum ramp, takeoff and landing weight | 1,150 kg (2,535 lb), equal for this example |
| Basic empty weight | 760 kg (1,676 lb), including full oil and unusable fuel |
| Available load before adding usable fuel | 390 kg (860 lb) |
| Usable fuel capacity | 190 L (50.2 US gal) |
| Assumed fuel density | 0.72 kg/L, approximately 6.0 lb/US gal |
| Combined baggage limit | 35 kg (77 lb), one compartment |

Full usable fuel weighs **190 × 0.72 = 136.8 kg (302 lb)**. That leaves **253.2 kg (558 lb)** for people and bags. Divide that by four and you get only **63.3 kg (140 lb) per occupant**, with no baggage allowance.

That calculation already tells a prospective buyer something concrete about this fictional airplane. Four lighter occupants on a short outing may fit; four larger adults travelling with overnight bags require a different fuel load or aircraft.

Real manuals may distinguish maximum ramp weight from maximum takeoff weight. Any extra ramp allowance is for ground operation under the published limits; it doesn't permit an overweight takeoff. Use the actual start and taxi fuel estimate, and check landing limits separately.

## Choose fuel for the trip

Full tanks can suit a long flight with fewer people. They can also consume carrying capacity you don't need for a shorter leg. Work out the required fuel first, then see whether the proposed load fits.

Build the fuel plan from start and taxi, climb, cruise, descent and arrival, using forecast winds and the airplane's documented consumption for the planned settings. Include the applicable reserve and your chosen allowance for uncertainty. A tank gauge or fuel-computer reading needs a sound starting quantity; reconcile it with the aircraft's approved fuel-checking method.

For an airplane beginning a flight in visual flight rules (VFR) conditions, [14 CFR §91.151](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-B/subject-group-ECFR4d5279ba676bedc/section-91.151) requires fuel to the first intended landing point, considering wind and forecast weather, plus at least 30 minutes by day or 45 minutes at night at normal cruising speed.

For an airplane operated in instrument flight rules (IFR) conditions, [§91.167](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-B/subject-group-ECFRef6e8c57f580cfd/section-91.167) requires destination fuel, alternate fuel unless its paragraph (b) exception applies, and then 45 minutes at normal cruising speed, considering weather reports, forecasts and conditions.

For airplanes, that exception requires an applicable standard instrument approach under Part 97, or a special approach issued to the operator, and appropriate weather information indicating a ceiling at least 610 m (2,000 ft) above airport elevation and visibility at least 4.8 km (3 statute miles) throughout one hour before to one hour after estimated arrival. It removes the alternate leg from that fuel requirement; the 45-minute reserve still applies.

Those are regulatory minimums, not a promise that the minimum will be comfortable when a runway closes or the wind changes. Decide the planning margin before trying to squeeze the group aboard.

## Three missions in the same airplane

Our group weighs **305 kg (672 lb)**: an 85 kg (187 lb) pilot, a 75 kg (165 lb) front passenger, and rear passengers of 80 kg (176 lb) and 65 kg (143 lb). Their bags weigh **25 kg (55 lb)**. Total payload is **330 kg (728 lb)**.

With the airplane empty, people and bags aboard, the weight is **760 + 330 = 1,090 kg (2,403 lb)**. Only **60 kg (132 lb)** remains below the ramp limit. At our assumed density, that allows **83.33 L (22.0 US gal)** of usable fuel on the ramp.

### A short day-VFR trip

Assume the fictional flight plan calls for 3 L of ground fuel, 45 L from takeoff to landing including climb and arrival, a 5 L contingency, and a 30 L reserve. That totals **83 L (21.9 US gal)**. At the assumed normal cruise flow of **30 L/h (7.9 US gal/h)**, the reserve represents one hour.

Ramp fuel weighs 59.76 kg (132 lb), giving **1,149.76 kg (2,535 lb)** on the ramp. Burning the estimated 3 L before takeoff reduces weight to **1,147.6 kg (2,530 lb)**. After the planned 45 L airborne burn, 35 L remains and landing weight is **1,115.2 kg (2,459 lb)**.

The arithmetic passes the example's weight limits. The ramp margin is just **0.24 kg (0.5 lb)**, though. An extra jacket or a small fuel-measurement error can erase it. A buyer should see this as a marginal mission fit, even before balance and performance checks.

### A longer trip with the same group

Keep the ground, contingency and reserve allowances, but increase airborne fuel to 85 L. Required ramp fuel becomes **123 L (32.5 US gal)**, weighing 88.56 kg (195 lb).

Ramp weight becomes **1,178.56 kg (2,598 lb)**; takeoff weight after the same ground burn is **1,176.4 kg (2,594 lb)**. Both exceed the example's limits. The landing weight might eventually be lower, but that doesn't authorize the overweight departure.

Reducing baggage alone won't fix this ramp calculation: all 25 kg of bags weigh less than the 28.56 kg excess. A fuel stop could make shorter legs possible if each revised leg has adequate fuel, loading and performance. It also changes travel time and adds another departure to evaluate.

### The short trip from a hot, elevated airport

Load the same 83 L and the same group, but depart at a pressure altitude of **1,524 m (5,000 ft)** in **30°C (86°F)** conditions. The weight figures still pass. That says nothing about whether the runway and terrain suit the departure.

The short-trip fuel estimate also needs rebuilding for the new climb. If that requires more fuel, the already tiny ramp margin disappears. You may need to reduce payload, choose another departure time or airport, or use an aircraft with more capacity and documented performance for the conditions.

| Mission | Ramp fuel | Ramp weight | Takeoff weight | Result of weight screening |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short trip | 83 L (21.9 US gal) | 1,149.76 kg (2,535 lb) | 1,147.6 kg (2,530 lb) | Fits with almost no ramp margin |
| Longer trip | 123 L (32.5 US gal) | 1,178.56 kg (2,598 lb) | 1,176.4 kg (2,594 lb) | Exceeds ramp and takeoff limits |
| Hot-airport short trip | Initially 83 L (21.9 US gal) | Initially 1,149.76 kg (2,535 lb) | Initially 1,147.6 kg (2,530 lb) | Fuel and performance need recalculation |

## Check balance through the flight

Centre of gravity (CG) is the airplane's balance point. To calculate it, multiply each load by its **arm**, the distance from the manual's reference datum, to obtain a moment. Add the moments and divide by total weight. Follow the manual's units and any moment-index scaling consistently.

For the fictional short trip, assume these arms and a constant permitted CG range of **1.05–1.15 m (41.34–45.28 in)** at the weights used. These invented limits simplify the lesson; a real envelope may change with weight or operating category.

| Item at takeoff | Weight | Assumed arm | Moment, kg·m |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Empty airplane | 760 kg (1,676 lb) | 1.00 m (39.37 in) | 760.00 |
| Front occupants | 160 kg (353 lb) | 0.95 m (37.40 in) | 152.00 |
| Rear occupants | 145 kg (320 lb) | 1.80 m (70.87 in) | 261.00 |
| Bags | 25 kg (55 lb) | 2.30 m (90.55 in) | 57.50 |
| Takeoff fuel, 80 L (21.1 US gal) | 57.6 kg (127 lb) | 1.20 m (47.24 in) | 69.12 |
| Total | 1,147.6 kg (2,530 lb) | CG: moment ÷ weight | 1,299.62 |

Takeoff CG is **1,299.62 ÷ 1,147.6 = 1.1325 m (44.59 in)**. After burning the planned 45 L, the fuel moment falls to 30.24 kg·m. Landing CG is **1,260.74 ÷ 1,115.2 = 1.1305 m (44.51 in)**. Both fit the invented envelope.

Here the CG moves forward as fuel burns because the assumed fuel arm is behind the loaded CG. The direction and size of the shift depend on the actual tank locations and loading. Check the planned landing condition and other limiting conditions through the flight, following the aircraft's loading instructions. The FAA's [weight-and-balance chapter](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak/12_phak_ch10.pdf) describes these fuel and loading effects.

Baggage limits still apply when total weight and CG pass. Check each compartment's limit, any combined limit, floor loading and restraints. Our 25 kg fits the fictional 35 kg compartment limit. Moving a heavy bag to a different location requires an approved stowage location and a fresh calculation.

## Runway and climb performance come next

Use the applicable performance charts with their stated configuration, technique and conditions. Read the notes for wind, surface and slope corrections; distinguish ground roll from distance over an obstacle. High density altitude reduces performance, and published figures depend on achieving the specified technique and aircraft condition. The FAA's [Chapter 11 on aircraft performance](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/13_phak_ch11.pdf) explains those dependencies.

Climb rate alone doesn't answer a terrain question. Suppose an entirely hypothetical steady climb gives **122 m/min (400 ft/min)** at a groundspeed of **167 km/h (90 kt)**. Groundspeed is 1.5 NM/min, so the climb gradient is **400 ÷ 1.5 = 267 ft/NM**, approximately 4.4%.

That calculation starts from assumed steady-climb numbers. It doesn't account for takeoff roll, transition to climb, acceleration or changing performance with height, and cannot establish obstacle clearance for a departure. Compare the actual route's demands with the aircraft's documented capability and your planning margins.

[§91.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-B/subject-group-ECFRe4c59b5f5506932/section-91.103) requires preflight familiarity with runway lengths and applicable takeoff and landing distance information. A buyer's mission assessment should cover the return flight too: different temperatures, baggage or fuel availability can change the result.

## A worksheet for comparing aircraft

Use one column per candidate aircraft and run the same realistic missions. Leave missing values blank until the seller or aircraft records establish them.

| Worksheet field | What to record or calculate |
| --- | --- |
| Aircraft identity and documents | Registration, serial number, configuration, manual revision, supplements and weight-record date |
| Empty-weight basis | Weight, CG or moment, included oil/fuel and installed equipment |
| Weight limits | Ramp, takeoff and landing limits; any other applicable restriction |
| Occupants | Actual clothed weights and proposed seats |
| Baggage | Weighed bags, compartment allocation and limits |
| Ramp fuel | Ground + planned airborne burn + applicable alternate + reserve + chosen contingency |
| Loaded weights | Ramp; takeoff after ground burn; landing after planned airborne burn |
| Balance | CG at takeoff, landing and other limiting conditions; correct envelope |
| Departure performance | Expected weather, runway/surface, obstacles, chart result and chosen margin |
| Arrival and return | Landing performance, refuelling plan and revised return loading |
| Unresolved evidence | Missing records, unverified equipment or unsupported performance assumptions |

When you [compare aircraft for sale](/aircraft), ask for the records behind the advertised carrying capacity. A lighter example with the equipment you need may suit your group better than a heavier, extensively equipped one. Engine history deserves its own assessment; [low hours and an old overhaul](/blog/low-engine-hours-old-overhaul) answer a different part of the buying decision.

Choose a candidate that carries your recurring trips with credible fuel and performance margins. If four adults and overnight bags are the normal mission, make that loading case a requirement from the first shortlist.
