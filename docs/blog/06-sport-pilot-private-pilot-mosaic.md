# Sport pilot vs private pilot after MOSAIC: which airplane can you fly?

MOSAIC lets sport pilots fly a wider range of airplanes, including some four-seat, standard-category aircraft. You still need to check the particular airplane, your pilot privileges and the proposed flight separately. Four seats don't authorize a sport pilot to carry three passengers, and an airplane advertised as “MOSAIC eligible” doesn't establish your medical qualification or endorsements.

For a buyer, the first question is whether the aircraft's records support eligibility. For a returning private pilot, the first question may be which privileges you can exercise with your current medical qualification. Choosing the aircraft before answering those questions can produce a shortlist you can't use.

This guide covers United States airplane operations. Research was checked on **12 October 2026** against FAA guidance and the eCFR text displayed as current through **8 October 2026**. The worked cases are fictional screening examples, not determinations about an aircraft for sale or an individual pilot's medical eligibility.

## What changed, and when

MOSAIC means **Modernization of Special Airworthiness Certification**. Its changes arrived in stages. The FAA's [MOSAIC fact sheet](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/fact_sheets/MOSAIC_Fact_Sheet.pdf) identifies these effective dates:

| Effective date | Relevant changes |
| --- | --- |
| 22 October 2025 | Pilot training, certification and privileges; other changes including repairman and maintenance rules |
| 24 July 2026 | Airworthiness-certification requirements, removal of the former light-sport aircraft definition, and specified operating changes |

Both dates have passed. An explanation based only on the 2023 proposal, or one saying all changes are still awaiting implementation, doesn't describe the current position. Use [§61.316](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.316) for sport-pilot aircraft criteria and the current rules for the operation you plan.

The old 599 kg (1,320 lb) landplane weight ceiling and 222 km/h (120 KCAS) speed ceiling no longer define which airplanes a sport pilot can operate. The FAA explains their removal in [AC 61-146, section 2.1](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-146.pdf). Other aircraft limitations, pilot restrictions and endorsement requirements still apply.

## Separate the pilot certificate from aircraft certification

**Sport pilot** describes pilot privileges. **Light-sport category** describes an aircraft-certification framework. They answer different questions.

A standard-category airplane may meet the sport-pilot criteria without becoming a light-sport category aircraft. Conversely, a light-sport category certification label alone doesn't establish that a sport pilot can operate it. You must check the performance and design requirements for the pilot's operation.

The FAA's [sport-pilot guidance](https://www.faa.gov/licenses_certificates/airmen_certification/sport_pilot) directs pilots to Part 61 subpart J and AC 61-146. That is the starting point for pilot qualification; the aircraft's airworthiness certificate and operating documents establish its certification basis.

This separation also affects maintenance. Sport-pilot eligibility doesn't convert a standard aircraft's certification or grant its owner new repairman privileges. Buying an airplane you can fly doesn't, by itself, establish which maintenance you may perform or sign off.

## Check the airplane against the actual criteria

For airplanes, [§61.316(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.316) requires a maximum **VS1** of no more than **109 km/h (59 KCAS)** at maximum certificated takeoff weight and the most critical centre of gravity. VS1 here is the maximum stall speed or minimum steady flight speed without lift-enhancing devices. **KCAS** means knots calibrated airspeed.

That differs from a landing-configuration stall figure, often labelled VS0. It also differs from indicated airspeed. A listing's bare “stall speed: 55 knots” doesn't identify the configuration, correction or test condition needed to answer the rule's question.

The airplane must also have a maximum seating capacity of four people and, if it has a cabin, that cabin must be non-pressurized. The rule includes directional-control and controlled-descent requirements following power loss. Fixed landing gear and permitted propeller types are the baseline, with additional routes for retractable gear and manual controllable-pitch propellers under §61.316(b).

Use this initial screening table, then check the full rule and aircraft documents:

| Screening question | Evidence to obtain |
| --- | --- |
| What aircraft are we discussing? | Registration, exact model, serial number and certification basis |
| What is its qualifying VS1? | Applicable flight-manual data and airspeed calibration, with maximum-weight and critical-CG conditions established |
| What is its maximum seating capacity? | Certification and configuration records; count capacity, not today's occupants |
| Is the cabin non-pressurized? | Approved design and configuration information |
| Do its power-loss characteristics satisfy the rule? | Supporting certification/design documentation; don't infer this from engine count alone |
| Does it have retractable gear or a manual controllable-pitch propeller? | Installed configuration and the pilot's applicable training/endorsement route |
| Has it met the criteria since original certification? | Original certification data and alteration history |

Sport pilots also can't operate an aircraft requiring a type rating under [§61.315(c)(21)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.315). Passing the stall-speed screen isn't the entire eligibility decision.

## Why removing seats or adding a kit does not fix eligibility

The words **since its original certification** prevent several tempting shortcuts. FAA [AC 61-146, sections 2.2, 3.2.2 and 3.3.2](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-146.pdf), explains that removing seats from an originally larger-capacity aircraft doesn't establish sport-pilot eligibility. Nor can an aftermarket stall-reduction modification make an aircraft qualify if its original certificated stall speed failed the criterion.

A kit can still be a permitted modification to an aircraft that already met the original criteria. Its existence doesn't establish that history. The same records question applies to an advertised engine conversion: identify the original eligibility basis and the approved changes, rather than treating the modification's name as proof.

Flying with less fuel doesn't change the rule's maximum-weight test condition either. An airplane that stalls more slowly at today's light loading may still exceed the qualifying VS1 at maximum certificated takeoff weight and critical CG. The [maneuvering-speed article](/blog/maneuvering-speed-aircraft-weight) explains how weight changes stall-related speeds; the eligibility rule specifies which loading case counts.

## Check your endorsements and current privileges

Sport-pilot privileges need the appropriate category and class qualification. A pilot certificate alone doesn't account for every aircraft-specific or operation-specific requirement.

For a sport pilot seeking retractable-gear or manual controllable-pitch propeller privileges, [§61.331](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.331) provides routes using the applicable §61.31(e) training and endorsement, or the specified training and proficiency endorsement for that equipment. The paragraph also contains a particular prior-experience exception for some retractable-gear aircraft intended for operation on water. Have an authorized instructor check the applicable route against your logbook.

The former 120 KCAS ceiling disappeared, but the speed-related training framework didn't. FAA [AC 61-146, section 3.4](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-146.pdf), explains the retained endorsement requirements around a **VH of 161 km/h (87 KCAS)**. VH is maximum speed in level flight at maximum continuous power; it isn't the airplane's stall speed. Relevant controlled-airspace and towered-airport requirements also need checking.

Returning private pilots should read [§61.303's certificate-and-medical table](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.303). An appropriately rated private pilot using only the qualifying driver's-license route can exercise sport privileges without acquiring a new sport certificate, but must observe the applicable sport operating limits. Other requirements that apply to their certificate and operation still matter.

A medical certificate's expiration doesn't erase the private pilot certificate. It changes which operations the pilot may conduct with their remaining qualifications. Before returning to flight, check the applicable [flight-review requirements](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.56) and [recent passenger-carrying experience](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.57).

## The medical qualification question

For daytime sport privileges, a qualifying US driver's license can replace an FAA medical certificate. That route has conditions; it isn't permission to disregard a medical history or a condition affecting safe flight.

Under [§61.23(c)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.23) and [§61.303(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.303), a pilot who has applied for an FAA medical must have been found eligible at their most recent application. The most recently issued medical must not have been suspended or revoked, and the most recent special-issuance authorization must not have been withdrawn. The pilot must follow applicable driver's-license restrictions and must not know, or have reason to know, of a medical condition preventing safe operation.

A driver's license by itself doesn't resolve a deferred or otherwise unresolved FAA medical application. If that describes your situation, have an aviation medical examiner clarify your qualification before you act as pilot in command. Don't assume that changing the name of the privileges you intend to exercise changes the application history.

**BasicMed** is a separate qualification route with medical examination, course, history and operating requirements. The FAA's [BasicMed page](https://www.faa.gov/licenses_certificates/airmen_certification/basic_med) explains that eligible private pilots may exercise private privileges under it. An expired medical doesn't establish BasicMed compliance automatically, and a driver's license alone isn't BasicMed.

## Night flying needs a separate check

MOSAIC added a night route for sport pilots. Under [§61.329](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.329), the pilot needs three hours of night training in the relevant category and class, including a night cross-country landing at an airport at least **46 km (25 NM)** from departure for airplane training, and ten night takeoffs and ten full-stop landings. An authorized instructor must endorse the pilot's proficiency.

The pilot must also hold the required FAA medical certificate or satisfy the applicable BasicMed conditions and limitations. A driver's license alone, sufficient in a qualifying daytime case, doesn't meet the night rule. Using BasicMed at night doesn't expand the sport pilot's aircraft criteria or passenger allowance.

The training endorsement doesn't replace recent passenger-carrying experience or the aircraft's night-operating requirements. A night-capable panel and lights don't establish the pilot's night privileges.

## Compare the privileges with your mission

These are selected differences, not a complete list of restrictions. The [sport limits in §61.315](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.315), [private limits in §61.113](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.113) and the operation's other rules need to be read together.

| Planned use | Exercising sport privileges | Exercising private privileges |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carry friends or family | No more than one passenger, even in a four-seat airplane | Capacity depends on aircraft loading, approvals and applicable medical/operating conditions |
| Fly at night | Separate training, endorsement and medical/BasicMed conditions | Requires applicable training, qualification, currency and aircraft compliance |
| Fly under IFR | Not authorized by sport privileges | Requires the appropriate [instrument rating](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.3), [currency](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.57) and aircraft compliance |
| Use a larger aircraft | Must satisfy §61.316 and sport restrictions | Requires the appropriate ratings, endorsements and other applicable qualifications |
| Fly in furtherance of a business | Prohibited | Some incidental-business flying is permitted under §61.113's conditions |

Sport privileges also require visual reference to the surface, at least **4.8 km (3 statute miles)** flight and surface visibility, and compliance with the ceiling of **3,048 m (10,000 ft) MSL or 610 m (2,000 ft) AGL, whichever is higher**. Airspace weather requirements may demand more. These limits can affect a trip well before aircraft capacity becomes the issue.

For someone beginning single-engine airplane training under the standard Part 61 routes, [§61.313](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.313) specifies a 20-hour sport-pilot minimum. The ordinary private-pilot route in [§61.109(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-61.109) specifies 40 hours, subject to its stated exceptions. Those are minimum requirements, not guaranteed completion times or price quotations.

Choose training around the flights you intend to make. If your recurring mission includes several passengers or an instrument-rating path, private training may fit those goals. If you intend daytime flying with one companion in a qualifying aircraft, sport privileges may fit, provided aircraft access and qualification are established.

## Three fictional screening cases

The following assumptions describe invented airplanes and pilots. Each case deliberately tests one question; a pass doesn't establish every other requirement.

### A four-seat airplane with one companion

Assume the original certification records establish four seats, a non-pressurized cabin and VS1 of **106 km/h (57 KCAS)** under the required conditions. Other aircraft criteria are also established. The pilot is appropriately qualified for its fixed gear and propeller and intends a daytime flight with one passenger.

The stated aircraft screen and passenger count fit sport criteria. The pilot must still establish medical qualification, required endorsements, currency, loading and the legality of this flight. Adding a second passenger would fail the sport passenger limit, even though two seats remain vacant.

### A low landing stall speed, but a higher clean speed

Assume a seller highlights a landing-configuration stall speed of **102 km/h (55 KCAS)**. The original clean VS1 at maximum weight and critical CG is **115 km/h (62 KCAS)**.

The attractive landing number doesn't answer §61.316's test. The stated clean value exceeds 59 KCAS. Less fuel or a subsequent stall-reduction kit doesn't change the original-eligibility requirement.

### An eligible airplane and a returning private pilot

Assume an airplane's eligibility is established and a private pilot has the appropriate category and class rating, an expired medical, and a qualifying driver's license. They plan to carry their partner and two children.

The four seats don't make this a sport-privilege flight: three passengers exceed its one-passenger limit. The pilot would need to establish the appropriate qualification to exercise private privileges, along with the other conditions for the flight. A night endorsement under the sport route wouldn't solve the passenger issue either.

## A worksheet for aircraft ads and rental inquiries

[Download the aircraft-and-pilot screening worksheet](/blog/sport-pilot-mosaic-eligibility-worksheet.md). It's an original Markdown checklist you can copy into your notes. Keep the aircraft, pilot and operator checks separate, and leave unsupported answers marked unconfirmed.

| Check | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Aircraft identity | Registration, model, serial number and applicable manual revision |
| Original eligibility | Qualifying VS1 basis, maximum seating, cabin, design criteria and original-certification history |
| Installed configuration | Modifications, supplements, gear and propeller; unresolved evidence |
| Pilot privileges | Certificate, category/class, endorsements and the privileges to be exercised |
| Medical qualification | Applicable route and confirmed status; private medical details belong in private records |
| Currency | Flight review and relevant recent experience |
| Proposed flight | Passengers, day/night, airspace, weather, altitude and purpose |
| Operator and insurer | Written rental/club approval, checkout, pilot qualifications and coverage terms |
| Aircraft capability | Actual loading, balance, fuel and departure/arrival performance |

A regulation allowing a flight doesn't establish that a particular club will rent you its aircraft or that an insurer will cover the proposed pilot and operation. Ask the operator and insurer for their actual requirements; don't infer them from a forum report. No current rental policy or insurance quote is claimed here.

When you [browse aircraft for sale](/aircraft), request the records before treating an ad as an eligible candidate. For a Cessna 172/180 conversion, ask about the base model, original certification and approved alterations. A conversion name alone doesn't establish sport-pilot eligibility.

Then assess whether the airplane carries your real mission with credible fuel and performance margins. Our [four-seat loading guide](/blog/four-seats-full-fuel-aircraft-payload) walks through that calculation. Eligibility establishes which aircraft and operations you can consider; the mission calculation establishes whether a particular candidate can do the trip.
